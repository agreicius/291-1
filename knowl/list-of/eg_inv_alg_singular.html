Example 1.12.9. Inverse algorithm.

Use Procedure 1.12.6 to decide whether the matrix
\begin{equation*} A=\begin{amatrix}[rrr]1\amp 2\amp 3\\ 4\amp 5\amp 6\\ 7\amp 8\amp 9 \end{amatrix} \end{equation*}
is invertible.
🔗
Solution.
Note that since in any case we do not half to produce an actual inverse, we can dispense with the super-sized augmented matrix described in Procedure 1.12.6, and instead just row reduce \(A\) to row echelon form. We have
\begin{align*} \begin{amatrix}[rrr]1\amp 2\amp 3\\ 4\amp 5\amp 6\\ 7\amp 8\amp 9 \end{amatrix} \amp \xrightarrow{r_2-4r_1} \begin{amatrix}[rrr]1\amp 2\amp 3\\ 0\amp -3\amp -6\\ 7\amp 8\amp 9 \end{amatrix} \\ \amp \xrightarrow{r_3-7r_1} \begin{amatrix}[rrr]1\amp 2\amp 3\\ 0\amp -3\amp -6\\ 0\amp -6\amp -12 \end{amatrix} \amp \\ \amp \xrightarrow{-\tfrac{1}{3}r_2} \begin{amatrix}[rrr]1\amp 2\amp 3\\ 0\amp 1\amp 2\\ 0\amp -6\amp -12 \end{amatrix}\\ \amp \xrightarrow{r_3+6r_2} \begin{amatrix}[rrr]\boxed{1}\amp 2\amp 3\\ 0\amp \boxed{1}\amp 2\\ 0\amp 0\amp 0 \end{amatrix}\text{.} \end{align*}
Since the row echelon form of \(A\) has no leading one in the third column, we conclude that \(A\) is not invertible.
