    Section 1.12 The invertibility theorem

    We are now in a position to prove our first big theorem: the invertibilty theorem. Logically speaking, the theorem states that a number of conditions are all equivalent to a matrix \(A\) being invertible. The advantage of having such a diverse collection of equivalent conditions is that we are provided with a powerful tool for determining when a matrix is invertible, and for computing the inverse matrix when it is.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.12.1 The invertibility theorem

    Theorem 1.12.1. Invertibility theorem.

    Let \(A\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\text{.}\) The following statements are equivalent.
    1. \(A\) is invertible.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The matrix equation
      \begin{equation*} A\underset{n\times 1}{\boldx}=\underset{n\times 1}{\boldb} \end{equation*}
      has a unique solution for any \(\boldb\in \R^n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The matrix equation
      \begin{equation*} A\underset{n\times 1}{\boldx}=\underset{n\times 1}{\boldzero} \end{equation*}
      has a unique solution: namely, \(\boldx=\boldzero_{n\times 1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(A\) is row equivalent to \(I_n\text{,}\) the \(n\times n\) identity matrix.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(A\) is a product of elementary matrices.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Recall that to show two statements \(\mathcal{P}\) and \(\mathcal{Q}\) are equivalent, we must show two implications: \(\mathcal{P}\implies \mathcal{Q}\text{,}\) and \(\mathcal{Q}\implies \mathcal{P}\text{.}\) Instead of doing this for each possible pair of sentences above, we ease our work load by instead showing the following cycle of implications:
    \begin{equation*} (1)\implies(2)\implies(3)\implies(4)\implies(5)\implies (1)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Since implication is transitive, starting at any point in our cycle and making our way around the chain of implications, we see that any one of the propositions implies any other proposition.
    🔗
    \((1)\implies (2)\).
    Suppose \(A^{-1}\) exists. Given any column vector \(\boldb\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{align*} A\boldx=\boldb \amp \iff \boldx=A^{-1}\boldb \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/cor_solving_invertible.html}{\text{Corollary 1.11.4}})\text{,} \end{align*}
    which shows that \(\boldx=A^{-1}\boldb\) is the unique solution to \(A\boldx=\boldb\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    \((2)\implies (3)\).
    Clearly, if \(A\boldx=\boldb\) has a unique solution for any choice of \(\boldb\text{,}\) then it has a unique solution for the particular choice \(\boldb=\boldzero\text{.}\) Since \(\boldx=\boldzero_{n\times 1}\) is clearly a solution to the equation, it must be the only solution.
    🔗
    🔗
    \((3)\implies (4)\).
    Row reduce \(A\) to a matrix \(U\) in reduced row echelon form using Gauss-Jordan elimination. (See Theorem 1.8.15.) Since the set of solutions to \(A\boldx=\boldzero\) is identical to the set of solutions to \(U\boldx=\boldzero\) (apply Theorem 1.7.12 to their corresponding linear systems), we see that \(\boldzero\) is the only solution to \(U\boldx=\boldzero\text{.}\) Procedure 1.8.21 now implies \(U\) has a leading one in each column. Since \(U\) is \(n\times n\) and in reduced row echelon form, it follows that \(U\) must be the identity matrix. (Convince yourself of this.) Thus \(A\) is row equivalent to \(U=I\text{,}\) the identity matrix.
    🔗
    🔗
    \((4)\implies (5)\).
    If \(A\) is row equivalent to \(I\text{,}\) then according to our discussion after (1.11.9), we have \(I=E_rE_{r-1}\cdots E_1A\) for some collection of elementary matrices \(E_i\text{.}\) Since elementary matrices are invertible we can multiply both sides of this equation by
    \begin{equation*} (E_rE_{r-1}\cdots E_1)^{-1}=E_1^{-1}E_{2}^{-1}\cdots E_r^{-1} \end{equation*}
    to conclude
    \begin{equation*} A=E_1^{-1}E_{2}^{-1}\cdots E_r^{-1}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Since inverses of elementary matrices are elementary (Theorem 1.11.19), we conclude that \(A\) is a product of elementary matrices.
    🔗
    🔗
    \((5)\implies (1)\).
    If \(A\) is a product of elementary matrices, then it is a product of invertible matrices. Since products of invertible matrices are invertible, we conclude that \(A\) is invertible.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.12.2.

    The invertibility theorem has an immediate application to linear systems where the number of equations is equal to the number of unknowns. In this special situation, the system is equivalent to a matrix equation of the form
    \begin{equation*} A\boldx=\boldb\text{,} \end{equation*}
    where \(A\) is a square matrix. According to the theorem, if we know \(A\) is invertible, then the matrix equation, and hence the linear system, has a unique solution: namely, \(\boldx=A^{-1}\boldb\text{.}\)
    🔗
    What if \(A\) is not invertible? Then the theorem only tells us that there is some column vector \(\boldc\text{,}\) not necessarily the given \(\boldb\text{,}\) such that the equation
    \begin{equation*} A\boldx=\boldc \end{equation*}
    does not have a unique solution. In other words, the theorem alone doesn’t allow us to conclude whether the given \(A\boldx=\boldb\) has a solution, and we must resort to our usual Gaussian elimination procedure to answer this question.
    🔗
    🔗
    The family of triangular matrices (upper, lower, and diagonal) defined below provides an easy testing ground for our new invertibility theorem.
    🔗

    Definition 1.12.3. Diagonal and triangular matrices.

    Let \(A=[a_{ij}]\) be \(n\times n\text{.}\)
    1. For each \(1\leq i\leq n\) the entry \(a_{ii}\) is called the \(i\)-th diagonal entry of \(A\text{,}\) and subarray of \(A\) consisting of \(a_{11}, a_{22}, \dots, a_{nn}\) is called the diagonal. An off-diagonal entry of \(A\) is any entry not among the diagonal entries.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The matrix \(A\) is diagonal if all off-diagonal entries are zero: i.e., if \(a_{ij}=0\) for all \(1\leq i,j\leq n\) with \(i\ne j\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The matrix \(A\) is upper triangular if all entries below the diagonal are zero: i.e., if \(a_{ij}=0\) for all \(1 leq j\lt i\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The matrix \(A\) is lower triangular if all entries above the diagonal are zero: i.e., if \(a_{ij}=0\) for all \(1\leq i\lt j \text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. The matrix \(A\) is triangular if \(A\) is upper triangular or lower triangular.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.12.4. Triangular \(3\times 3\) matrices.

    The set \(W_1\) of all diagonal \(3\times 3\) matrices can be described as
    \begin{equation*} W_1=\left\{ \begin{amatrix}[ccc]r\amp 0\amp 0\\ 0\amp s\amp 0 \\ 0\amp 0\amp t \end{amatrix}\colon r,s,t\in \R\right\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The set \(W_2\) of all upper triangular \(3\times 3\) matrices can be described as
    \begin{equation*} W_2=\left\{ \begin{amatrix}[ccc]r\amp s\amp t\\ 0\amp u\amp v \\ 0\amp 0\amp w \end{amatrix}\colon r,s,t,u,v,w\in \R\right\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The set \(W_3\) of all lower triangular \(3\times 3\) matrices can be described as
    \begin{equation*} W_3=\left\{ \begin{amatrix}[ccc]r\amp 0\amp 0\\ s\amp t\amp 0 \\ u\amp v\amp w \end{amatrix}\colon r,s,t,u,v,w\in \R\right\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.12.5. Invertibility of triangular matrices.

    Let \(A=[a_{ij}]\) be a triangular \(n\times n\) matrix. Then \(A\) is invertible if and only if \(a_{ii}\ne 0\) for all \(1\leq i\leq n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In other words, \(A\) is invertible if and only if the diagonal entries of \(A\) are all nonzero.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Subsection 1.12.2 Invertibility algorithms

    The proof of the implication \((4)\implies (5)\) of Theorem 1.12.1 can be expanded into an algorithm that (1) decides whether a given matrix \(A\) is invertible, and (2) computes \(A^{-1}\) if \(A\) is invertible.
    🔗

    Procedure 1.12.6. Inverse algorithm.

    Let \(A\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\) To test for invertibility of \(A\) proceed as follows.
    1. Build the \(n\times 2n\) augmented matrix \(\begin{amatrix}[c|c]A\amp I_n\end{amatrix}\) and use Gaussian elimination to row reduce to the form \(\begin{amatrix}[c|c]U\amp B\end{amatrix} \text{,}\) where \(U\) is in row echelon form.
      🔗
      The matrix \(A\) is invertible if and only if \(U\) has \(n\) leading ones.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(U\) has \(n\) leading ones, row reduce further to a matrix of the form \(\begin{amatrix}[c|c] I_n\amp C\end{amatrix}\text{.}\) Then \(A^{-1}=C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    From the proof of Theorem 1.12.1 we know \(A\) is invertible if and only if \(U\) has \(n\) leading ones. The question remains as to why reducing the augmented matrix \(\begin{amatrix}[c|c]A\amp I\end{amatrix}\) to \(\begin{amatrix}[c|c]I\amp C\end{amatrix}\) tells us that \(A^{-1}=C\text{.}\) Let \(E_1, E_2, \dots, E_r\) be the elementary matrices representing the row operations involved in this process. Then we have
    \begin{equation*} E_rE_{r-1}\cdots E_1A=I_n\text{.} \end{equation*}
    After a little algebra, we see that
    \begin{equation*} A^{-1}=E_rE_{r-1}\cdots E_1\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Since \(C\) is the result of applying same row operations to \(I_n\) we have
    \begin{equation*} C=E_rE_{r-1}\cdots E_1I=E_rE_{r-1}\cdots E_1=A^{-1}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    as claimed.
    🔗
    🔗
    From the proof of Theorem 1.12.1 we also derive an algorithm for writing an invertible matrix as a product of elementary matrices. You should think of this as a first example of the great versatility of Gaussian elimination, as conveyed by our Mantra 1.8.10.
    🔗

    Procedure 1.12.7. Product of elementary matrices algorithm.

    Let \(A\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\) To (potentially) write \(A\) as a product of elementary matrices, proceed as follows.
    1. Attempt to row reduce \(A\) to the identity, keeping track of your sequence of row operations in the form of elementary matrices.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If you are able to row reduce \(A\) to \(I_n\) with a sequence of row operatons corresponding to the elementary matrices \(E_1, E_2, \dots, E_r\text{,}\) then
      \begin{equation*} A=E_1^{-1}E_2^{-1}\cdots E_{r}^{-1}. \end{equation*}
      Since the inverse of an elementary matrix is elementary, we have written \(A\) as a product of elementary matrices.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    See the proof of the implication \((4)\implies (5)\) in Theorem 1.12.1.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.12.8. Inverse algorithms.

    Let
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{amatrix}[rrr]1\amp 2\amp 0\\ 1\amp 2\amp 1\\ -1\amp -1\amp 3 \end{amatrix} \text{.} \end{equation*}
    Combining both algorithms we can decide whether \(A\) is invertible, and if so, compute \(A^{-1}\) and write \(A\) as a product of elementary matrices.

    Subsection 1.12.3 Some theoretical loose ends

    The two inveribility algorithms above are nice examples of how a theoretical result like our invertibility theorem can pay some serious computational dividends. Namely, thanks to the theory we have discovered a method of computing the inverse of a matrix that essentially boils down to row reduction.
    🔗
    We finish this section with a number of theoretical implications that tie up some loose ends. The results below are all consequences in some way of Theorem 1.12.1. The fist shows that in fact one one of the defining equalities \(AB=I\) or \(BA=I\) suffices to define the inverse of a matrix.
    🔗

    Corollary 1.12.12. Left-inverse if and only if right-inverse.

    Let \(A\) and \(B\) be \(n\times n\text{.}\) Then
    1. \(BA=I_n\implies AB=I_n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(AB=I_n\implies BA=I_n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    In plain English: a matrix \(B\) is a left-inverse of \(A\) if and only if it is a right-inverse of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    \begin{equation*} A \text{ and } B \text{ invertible }\implies AB \text{ invertibe} \end{equation*}
    to an equivalence.

    Let \(A\) and \(B\) be \(n\times n\text{.}\) Then \(AB\) is invertible if and only if \(A\) and \(B\) are both invertible: i.e.,
    \begin{equation*} A \text{ and } B \text{ invertible }\iff AB \text{ invertible} \end{equation*}

    Corollary 1.12.14. Row equivalence and invertible matrices.

    Let \(A\) and \(B\) be \(m\times n\) matrices. The following statements are equivalent.

    Corollary 1.12.16. Uniqueness of reduced row echelon form.

    Any matrix \(A\) is row equivalent to a unique matrix in reduced row echelon form.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Let \(A\) be an \(m\times n\) matrix. Using Gauss-Jordan elimination, we can row reduce \(A\) to a matrix \(U\) in reduced row echelon form. Suppose \(A\) is also row equivalent to the matrix \(V\) in reduced row echelon form; then \(U\) and \(V\) are row equivalent, since the row equivalence relation is symmetric and transitive. Thus it suffices to show that if \(U\) and \(V\) are row equivalent matrices in reduced row echelon form, then \(U=V\text{.}\) We do so by induction on \(n\text{.}\) The base step is trivial, since there are only two \(m\times 1\) matrices in reduced row echelon form: namely \(\boldzero_{m\times 1}\) and the column vector with a one in the first row and zeros elsewhere.
    🔗
    For the induction step we assume that any two row equivalent \(m\times n\) matrices in reduced row echelon form are equal. Let \(U=[u_{ij}]\) and \(V=[v_{ij}]\) be row equivalent \(m\times (n+1)\) matrices in reduced row echelon form, and let \(\boldu_j\) and \(\boldv_j\) denote the columns of \(U\) and \(V\text{,}\) respectively. By Corollary 1.12.14 there is an invertible matrix \(Q\) such that \(QU=U'\text{.}\) The first \(n\) columns of \(U\) and \(V\) form matrices \(U'\) and \(V'\text{,}\) respectively, that are in reduced row echelon form, as one easily checks. Furthermore, \(U'\) and \(V'\) are row equivalent: indeed, since \(Q\boldu_j=\boldv_j\) for all \(1\leq j\leq n+1\text{,}\) we see that \(QU'=V'\text{,}\) and thus \(U'\) and \(V'\) are row equivalent. By the induction hypothesis we must have \(U'=V'\text{,}\) and it remains only to show that \(\boldu_{n+1}=\boldv_{n+1}\text{.}\) We treat two cases: the case where one of \(U\) and \(V\) has a no leading one in its last column, and the case where both matrices have a leading one in their last column.
    🔗
    In the first case, suppose without loss of generality that the last column of \(U\) does not have a leading one. Consider the linear system with augmented matrix \([U\mid \boldx]\text{.}\) Since the last column of \(U\) is free, it follows from Procedure 1.8.21 that for all \(t\in \R\) there is a solution to this system of the form \(\boldx=(f_1(t),f_2(t),\dots, f_n(t),t)\text{,}\) where \(f_j(t)\) is some function of \(t\text{.}\) Since \(U\) and \(V\) are row equivalent, so are \([U\mid \boldzero]\) and \([V\mid \boldzero]\text{,}\) and thus the two systems corresponding to these augmented matrices have the same solution sets. It follows that for all \(t\in \R\text{,}\) we have \(U\boldx_t=V\boldx_t=\boldzero\text{.}\) Using the column method, we conclude that
    \begin{equation*} f_1(t)\boldu_1+\cdots +f_{n}(t)\boldu_n+t\bold_{n+1}=f_1(t)\boldv_1+\cdots +f_{n}(t)\boldv_n+t\bold_{n+1} \end{equation*}
    for all \(t\in \R\text{.}\) Since \(U'=V'\text{,}\) we have \(\boldu_j=\boldv_j\) for all \(1\leq j\leq n\text{,}\) and thus conclude that \(t\boldu_{n+1}=t\boldv_{n_1}\) for all \(t\in \R\text{.}\) Taking \(t=1\text{,}\) we conclude that \(\boldu_{n+1}=\boldv_{n+1}\text{,}\) and hence that \(U=V\) in this case.
    🔗
    Finally, we consider the case where \(U\) and \(V\) both have leading ones in their last columns. Since \(U\) and \(U'\) are in reduced row echelon form, it follows that the leading one in the last column of \(U\) occurs in the row of \(U\) whose corresponding row in \(U'\) is the first zero row of \(U'\text{.}\) Since the same statement mutatis mutandi applies to \(V\) and \(V'\text{,}\) and since \(U'=V'\text{,}\) we see that the leading ones in the last columns of \(U\) and \(V\) occur in the same row. Since all other entries in that last column must be zero, we conclude that \(\boldu_{n+1}=\boldv_{n+1}\text{,}\) and hence that \(U=V\) in this case as well.

    11.
    \(A=\begin{amatrix}[rr]0\amp 1\\2\amp -5 \end{amatrix}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Row reduce \(A\) to the identity matrix:
    \begin{align*} \begin{amatrix}[rr]0\amp 1\\2\amp -5 \end{amatrix} \amp \xrightarrow[]{r_1\leftrightarrow r_1} \begin{amatrix}[rr]2\amp -5 \\0\amp 1 \end{amatrix}\\ \amp \xrightarrow[]{\frac{1}{2}r_1} \begin{amatrix}[rr]1\amp -5/2\\ 0\amp 1 \end{amatrix}\\ \amp \xrightarrow[]{r_1+\frac{5}{2}r_2} \begin{amatrix}[rr]1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 1 \end{amatrix} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The corresponding elementary matrices are
    \begin{equation*} E_1 = \begin{amatrix}[rr]0\amp 1\\ 1\amp 0 \end{amatrix} , E_2 = \begin{amatrix}[rr]1/2\amp 0\\ 0\amp 1 \end{amatrix}, E_3=\begin{amatrix}[rr]1\amp 5/2\\ 0\amp 1 \end{amatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    It follows that \(E_3E_2E_1A=I\text{,}\) and hence that
    \begin{equation*} A=E_1^{-1}E_{2}^{-1}E_3^{-1}=\begin{amatrix}[rr]0\amp 1\\ 1\amp 0 \end{amatrix} \begin{amatrix}[rr]2\amp 0\\ 0\amp 1 \end{amatrix} \begin{amatrix}[rr]1\amp -5/2\\ 0\amp 1 \end{amatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
