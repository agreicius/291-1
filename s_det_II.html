    Section 1.14 Further properties of the determinant

    Subsection 1.14.1 Determinant is alternating and multilinear

    For the next theorem, it is convenient to think of the determinant a function of the \(n\) rows \(\boldr_1,\boldr_2,\dots, \boldr_n\) of an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\text{.}\) That is, instead of thinking of \(\det\) as a function \(\det\colon \ML_{n,n}(F)\rightarrow F\text{,}\) setting \(V=F^n\text{,}\) we will think of it as a function
    \begin{align*} \det\colon V^n \amp \rightarrow F \\ (\boldr_1,\boldr_2,\dots, \boldr_n) \amp \mapsto \det(\boldr_1,\boldr_2,\dots, \boldr_n)\text{.} \end{align*}
    To facilitate arguments in this vein, we will adopt temporarily that convention of writing a matrix \(A\) whose \(i\)-th row is \(\boldr_i\) as
    \begin{equation*} A=(\boldr_1,\boldr_2,\dots, \boldr_n)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Yes, we are taking some notational liberties here, but we will drop this convention after this section is complete!
    🔗

    Theorem 1.14.1. Determinant: alternating,skew-symmetric, multilinear.

    Let \(F\) be a field, let \(n\) be a positive integer, and let \(V=F^n\text{.}\) We consider \(\det\) as a function on \(V^n\) by defining \(\det(\boldr_1,\boldr_2,\dots, \boldr_n)\) as \(\det A\text{,}\) where \(A=(\boldr_1,\boldr_2,\dots, \boldr_n)\text{.}\)
    1. Alternating property.
      If \((\boldr_1,\boldr_2,\dots, \boldr_n)\in V\) satisfies \(\boldr_i=\boldr_j\) for some indices \(i\ne j\text{,}\) then \(\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_n)=0\text{.}\) In terms of matrices, if a matrix \(A\) has two identical rows, then \(\det A=0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Multilinear property.
      Given any \((r_i)_{i=1}^n\in V^n\text{,}\) and index \(1\leq k\leq n\text{,}\) and any vector \(\bolds_i\in F^n\text{,}\) and scalar \(c\in F\text{,}\) we have
      \begin{align} \det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i+\bolds_i,\dots, \boldr_n)\amp =\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_n)+\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \bolds_i,\dots, \boldr_n)\tag{1.14.1}\\ \det(\boldr_1,\dots, c\boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_n)\amp =c\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_n)\text{.}\tag{1.14.2} \end{align}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Skew-symmetric.
      Suppose \(1\leq i< j\leq n\text{.}\) We have
      \begin{equation} \det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_n)=-\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots,\boldr_n)\text{.}\tag{1.14.3} \end{equation}
      In terms of matrices, if \(B\) is the result of swapping two rows of a matrix \(A\text{,}\) then \(\det B=-\det A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    1. The alternating property is true vacuously for \(n=1\text{,}\) and can be easily verified for \(n=2\) via a direct computation. An induction argument then easily proves the result for all \(n\geq 2\) by expanding along a row of the matrix \(A\) that is not equal to \(\boldr_i\) or \(\boldr_j\text{.}\) We leave this to the reader.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We can prove the multilinear properties of (2) directly by expanding along the \(k\)-th row of the corresponding matrix. In more detail, let \(A=(\boldr_1,\boldr_2,\dots, \boldr_n)\text{,}\) where \(\boldr_i=(r_{i,1},\dots, r_{i,n})\) for all \(i\text{,}\) and let \(B=(\boldr_1,\dots, \bolds_i, \dots, \boldr_n)\text{,}\) where \(\bolds_i=(s_{k,1},s_{k,2},\dots, s_{k,n})\text{.}\) Letting \(C=(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i+\bolds_i,\dots, \boldr_n)\text{,}\) we have
      \begin{gather*} \det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i+\bolds_i,\dots, \boldr_n) =\det C=\\ \sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^{k+j}(r_{i,j}+s_{i,j})\det C_{i,j}=\\ \sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^{k+j}r_{i,j}\det C_{i,j}+\sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^{k+j}s_{i,j}\det C_{i,j}=\\ \sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^{k+j}r_{i,j}\det A_{i,j}+ \sum_{j=1}^n(-1)^{k+j}s_{i,j}\det B_{i,j}=\\ \det A+\det B=\\ \det(\boldr_1,\dots,\boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_n)+\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \bolds_i,\dots, \boldr_n) \text{,} \end{gather*}
      where we have used the fact that \(C_{i,j}=A_{i,j}=B_{i,j}\) as these three matrices are identical except for their \(k\)-th rows.
      🔗
      Similarly, we have
      \begin{align*} \det (\boldr_1,\dots, c\boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_n) \amp = \sum_{j=1}^n(-1)^{k+j}cr_{i,j}\det A_{i,j}\\ \amp = c\sum_{j=1}^n(-1)^{k+j}r_{i,j}\det A_{i,j} \\ \amp = c\det(\boldr_1,\boldr_2,\dots, \boldr_n) \text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The skew-symmetric property now follows from the alternating and multilinear properties. Given \(1\leq i< j\leq n\text{,}\) we have
      \begin{gather*} 0 =\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \underset{i-\text{th entry}}{\boldr_i+\boldr_j},\dots, \underset{j-\text{th entry}}{\boldr_i+\boldr_j},\dots, \boldr_n) \text{,} \end{gather*}
      by the alternating property. Expanding out the right side of the equation above using the multilinear property in succession yields
      \begin{gather*} \det(\boldr_1,\dots,\boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots,\boldr_n)+\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_n)\\ +\det(\boldr_1,\dots,\boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots,\boldr_n) +\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_n) \\ =\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_n)+\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_n)\text{,} \end{gather*}
      since
      \begin{equation*} \det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots,\boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_n)=\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_n)=0 \end{equation*}
      by the alternating property again. In all, we have
      \begin{equation*} 0=\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_n)+\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_n)\text{,} \end{equation*}
      or equivalently,
      \begin{equation*} \det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_n)=-\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_n)\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The two properties (1.14.1)–(1.14.2) taken together are summarized by saying the determinant is linear in each row, and hence multilinear overall. Note that by using the two properties in succession, we have
    \begin{equation} \det(\boldr_1,\dots, c\boldr_i+d\bolds_i,\dots, \boldr_n)=c\det(\boldr_1,\dots,\boldr_i,\dots \boldr_n)+d\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \bolds_i,\dots, \boldr_n)\tag{1.14.4} \end{equation}
    for all \(c,d\in F\text{.}\) But what do these properties mean about the determinant of matrices? Property (1.14.2) is easy enough to express in terms of matrices: if you scale a single row of a matrix \(A\) by \(c\text{,}\) then the value of the determinant is scaled by \(c\text{.}\) Property (1.14.1), on the other hand, is a little more convoluted. Its treatment in the proof of Theorem 1.14.1 probably is the best articulation of this: if
    \begin{align*} A\amp =(\boldr_1,\dots,\boldr_i,\dots \boldr_n)\\ B\amp =(\boldr_1,\dots, \bolds_i,\dots, \boldr_n)\\ C\amp =(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i+\bolds_i,\dots, \boldr_n)\text{,} \end{align*}
    then
    \begin{equation*} \det C=\det A+\det B\text{.} \end{equation*}
    That ends up being a very powerful property of the determinant. In particular, when we pick \(\bolds_i\) to be \(c\boldr_j\text{,}\) for some other index \(i\text{,}\) we conclude that
    \begin{align*} \det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_i+c\boldr_j) \amp = \det(\boldr_1,\dots,\boldr_i, \boldr_n)+c\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_j,\dots, \boldr_n) \\ \amp=\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_n)+0 \\ \amp =\det(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_n)\text{,} \end{align*}
    where the second to last equality follows from the fact that the matrix obtained by replacing the \(i\)-th row with the \(j\)-th row has two identical rows, and hence determinant equal to zero. We have just shown that the row addition operation leaves the determinant of a matrix unchanged! Add to this the skew-symmetric property of the determinant, and our comment above about scaling single rows, and we have the following complete description of the effect of row operations on the determinant. For simplicity, we express the result in terms of elementary matrices multiplied on the left: recall that this is the same thing as applying an elementary row operation.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.14.2. Row operations and determinant.

    Let \(A\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\text{.}\)
    1. For all \(1\leq i\leq n\) and \(c\in F\text{,}\) we have
      \begin{equation*} \det(\underset{c\cdot r_i}{E}\, A)=c\det(A)\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. For all \(1\leq i< j\leq n\text{,}\) we have
      \begin{equation*} \det(\underset{r_i\leftrightarrow r_j}{E}\, A)=-\det(A) \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. For all \(1\leq i,j\leq n\) and \(c\in F\text{,}\) we have
      \begin{equation*} \det(\underset{r_i+cr_j}{E}\, A)=\det(A)\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    In particular, taking \(A=I\) above, we derive the following determinant formulas for elementary matrices:
    \begin{align} \det \underset{c\, r_i}{E} \amp =c \amp \det\underset{r_i\leftrightarrow r_j}{E}\amp=-1 \amp \det\underset{r_i+cr_j}{E}\amp=1\text{.}\tag{1.14.5} \end{align}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.14.3.

    In the language of row operations, Theorem 1.14.2 translates as follows:
    • Scaling a row of a matrix by \(c\) has the effect of scaling the determinant by \(c\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Swapping two rows of a matrix changes the sign of the determinant.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Performing a row addition operation on a matrix has no effect on the determinant.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Corollary 1.14.4. Determinant and products of elementary matrices.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\) matrix, and suppose we have
    \begin{equation} B=E_rE_{r-1}\cdots E_1\, A\tag{1.14.6} \end{equation}
    for some collection of elementary matrices \(E_i\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation} \det B=\det E_r\, \det E_{r-1}\cdots \det E_1\, \det A\text{.}\tag{1.14.7} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    This is an easy proof by induction on the number \(r\) of elementary matrices involved, the base case (\(r=1\)) of which is covered by Theorem 1.14.2.
    🔗
    🔗
    Corollary 1.14.4 has both computational and theoretical applications.
    🔗
    On the computational side, it suggests an alternative method of computing \(\det A\text{:}\) first row reduce \(A\) to a simpler matrix \(B\text{,}\) making sure to keep track of the operations you use; set up an equation as in (1.14.6) representing the row reduction; then solve the corresponding equation (1.14.7) for \(\det A\) in terms of \(\det B\) and the \(\det E_i\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 1.14.5. Determinant via row reduction.

    Suppose the matrix \(A\) can be row reduced to
    \begin{equation*} B=\begin{amatrix}[rrr]2\amp -7\amp -8\\ 0\amp -3\amp 4\\ 0\amp 0\amp 2 \end{amatrix} \end{equation*}
    by perfomring the following sequence of row operations:
    1. First swap the second and third rows.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Then scale the first row by \(3\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Then replace the second row with the second row plus the first row.
      🔗
      🔗
    Compute \(\det A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    In terms of elementary matrices we have
    \begin{equation*} B=\underset{r_2+r_1}{E}\underset{3r_1}{E}\underset{r_2\leftrightarrow r_3}{E}A, \end{equation*}
    and hence
    \begin{equation*} \det B=\det \underset{r_2+r_1}{E}\, \det \underset{3r_1}{E}\, \det \underset{r_2\leftrightarrow r_3}{E}\, \det A=(1)(3)(-1)\det A=(-3)\det A. \end{equation*}
    We conclude that
    \begin{equation*} \det A=-\frac{1}{3}\det B=-(1/3)(-12)=4\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    On the theoretical side, Corollary 1.14.4 implies both Theorem 1.14.6 and Theorem 1.14.7.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.14.6. Determinant and invertibility.

    Let \(A\) be an \(n\times n\) matrix. Then \(A\) is invertible if and only if \(\det A\ne 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    The implication \(\det A\ne 0\implies A \text{ invertible}\) was proved in Theorem 1.14.11.
    🔗
    For the other direction, assume \(A\) is invertible. Then Theorem 1.12.1 implies \(A\) is a product of elementary matrices:
    \begin{equation*} A=E_1E_2\cdots E_r\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Then Corollary 1.14.4 implies
    \begin{equation*} \det A=\det E_1\, \det E_2\cdots \det E_1\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Since \(\det E_i\ne 0\) for all \(i\) (Theorem 1.14.2), we conclude \(\det A\ne 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.14.7. Determinant is multiplicative.

    Let \(A\) and \(B\) be \(n\times n\) matrices. Then
    \begin{equation*} \det AB=\det A\, \det B\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We consider two cases based on the invertibility of \(A\) and/or \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(A\) or \(B\) is not invertible.
    In this case \(AB\) is not invertible (Corollary 1.12.13), and hence \(\det AB=0\) by Theorem 1.14.6. By the same reasoning we must have \(\det A=0\) or \(\det B=0\text{.}\) It follows that
    \begin{equation*} \det AB=\det A\, \det B=0 \end{equation*}
    in this case.
    🔗
    🔗
    \(A\) and \(B\) both invertible.
    In this case we can write
    \begin{align*} A \amp E_1E_2\cdots E_r \amp B\amp=E'_1E'_2\cdots E'_s \end{align*}
    for some elementary matrices \(E_i\) and \(E'_j\) (Theorem 1.12.1). Then
    \begin{align*} \det AB \amp= \det(E_1E_2\cdots E_rE'_1E'_2\cdots E'_s) \\ \amp= \det E_1\det E_2\cdots \det E_r\det E'_1\det E'_2\cdots \det E'_s \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/cor_det_prod_elem.html}{\text{Corollary 1.14.4}})\\ \amp=\det A\, \det B \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/cor_det_prod_elem.html}{\text{Corollary 1.14.4}}) \text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We end this section and chapter with a slight expansion of our invertibility theorem. The equivalence of statement 7 is a direct consequence of Theorem 1.14.6. The equivalence of the equivalence of statement 3 was given earlier as an exercise. (See Exercise 1.12.5.15.)
    🔗

    Theorem 1.14.8. Invertibility theorem.

    Let \(A=\) be an \(n\times n\) matrix. The following statements are equivalent.
    1. \(A\) is invertible.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The matrix equation
      \begin{equation*} A\underset{n\times 1}{\boldx}=\underset{n\times 1}{\boldb} \end{equation*}
      has a unique solution for any \(\boldb\in \R^n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The matrix equation
      \begin{equation*} A\underset{n\times 1}{\boldx}=\underset{n\times 1}{\boldb} \end{equation*}
      has a solution for any \(\boldb\in \R^n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The matrix equation
      \begin{equation*} A\underset{n\times 1}{\boldx}=\underset{n\times 1}{\boldzero} \end{equation*}
      has a unique solution: namely, \(\boldx=\boldzero_{n\times 1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(A\) is row equivalent to \(I_n\text{,}\) the \(n\times n\) identity matrix.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. \(A\) is a product of elementary matrices.
      🔗
      🔗
    7. \(\det A\ne 0\text{.}\)

    Subsection 1.14.2 Adjoint matrix

    Definition 1.14.9. Adjoint matrix.

    Let \(A\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\text{.}\) The adjoint matrix of \(A\text{,}\) denoted \(\adj A\text{,}\) is the \(n\times n\) matrix whose \(i,j\)-th entry is defined as follows:
    \begin{equation*} [\adj A]_{i,j}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{j,i}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.14.10.

    Be careful of the order reversal in this definition. The \(i,j\)-th entry of \(\adj A\) is equal to plus or minus the \(j,i\)-th minor of \(A\text{.}\) Let’s see this in action for some small matrices.
    🔗
    For
    \begin{equation*} A=\abcdmatrix{a}{b}{c}{d} \end{equation*}
    we have
    \begin{equation*} \adj A=\begin{amatrix}[rr] M_{11}\amp -M_{21}\\ -M_{12}\amp M_{22}\end{amatrix}= \begin{amatrix}[rr]d\amp -b\\ -c\amp a \end{amatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{bmatrix} a\amp b\amp c\\ d\amp e\amp f\\ g\amp h\amp i \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    we have
    \begin{equation*} \adj A=\begin{amatrix}[rrr] M_{1,1}\amp -M_{2,1}\amp M_{3,1}\\ -M_{1,2}\amp M_{2,2}\amp -M_{3,2}\\ M_{1,3}\amp -M_{2,3}\amp M_{3,3} \end{amatrix} =\begin{amatrix}[rrr] (ei-fh)\amp -(bi-ch)\amp (bf-ce)\\ -(di-fg)\amp (ai-cg)\amp -(af-cd)\\ (dh-eg)\amp -(ah-bg)\amp (ae-bd) \end{amatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.14.11. Adjoint matrix formula.

    Given \(A\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation} A\,(\adj A)=(\adj A)\, A=(\det A)I\text{.}\tag{1.14.8} \end{equation}
    As a consequence \(A\) is invertible if and only if \(\det A\ne 0\text{,}\) and in this case we have
    \begin{equation} A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det A}\, \adj A\text{.}\tag{1.14.9} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    First observe that the second statement regarding invertibility follows directly from (1.14.8), since in this case setting \(B=\frac{1}{\det A}\, \adj A\) we have
    \begin{equation*} AB=BA=\frac{1}{\det A}(A\adj A)=\frac{1}{\det A}((\det A)\, I)=I\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus it suffices to prove (1.14.8). To do so, we must show that
    \begin{equation*} [A\, \adj A]_{i,j}=[\adj A\, A]_{i,j}= \begin{cases} \det A\amp \text{if } i=j\\ 0 \amp \text{if } i\ne j \text{.}\end{cases} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Case: \(i=j\).
    In this case we have
    \begin{align*} [A\, \adj A]_{i,j} \amp =\sum_{k=1}^na_{ik}[\adj A]_{ki} \\ \amp =\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}((-1)^{i+k}M_{ik}) \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/d_adjoint.html}{\text{Definition 1.14.9}} )\\ \amp =\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{i+k}a_{ik}M_{ik} \\ \amp =\det A \text{.} \end{align*}
    A similar argument shows that \((\adj A\, A)_{ii}=\det A\text{,}\) though in this case we use expansion along a column.
    🔗
    🔗
    Case: \(i\ne j\).
    When \(i\ne j\) we have
    \begin{align*} [A\, \adj A]_{i,j} \amp =\sum_{k=1}^na_{ik}[\adj A]_{i,j} \\ \amp =\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}((-1)^{k+j}M_{j,k}) \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/d_adjoint.html}{\text{Definition 1.14.9}} )\\ \amp =\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{k+j}a_{ik}M_{j,k} \\ \amp =\det C \text{,} \end{align*}
    where \(C\) is the matrix obtained by replacing the \(j\)-th row of \(A\) with a copy of its \(i\)-th row. Since \(C\) has two identical rows [cross-reference to target(s) "th_det_zero_repeated_row" missing or not unique] implies
    \begin{equation*} [A\, \adj A]_{i,j}=\det C=0\text{,} \end{equation*}
    as desired. Once again, a similar argument using expansion along a column shows that \([\adj A\, A]_{ij}=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.14.12.

    Use the adjoint matrix formula to compute \(A^{-1}\text{,}\) where
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{amatrix}[rrr]1\amp 2\amp 1 \\ -1\amp 1\amp -1\\ 0\amp 2\amp 2 \end{amatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    First compute \(\det A=\) by expanding along the third row:
    \begin{equation*} \det A=-2(-1+1)+2(2+1)=6\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Next, compute
    \begin{equation*} \adj A=\begin{amatrix}[rrr] 4\amp -2 \amp -3\\ 2\amp 2\amp 0\\ -2\amp -2\amp 3 \end{amatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Then we have
    \begin{equation*} A^{-1}=\frac{1}{6}\,\adj A=\frac{1}{6}\, \begin{amatrix}[rrr] 4\amp -2 \amp -3\\ 2\amp 2\amp 0\\ -2\amp -2\amp 3 \end{amatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.14.13.

    Before you get too excited about the adjoint matrix formula, you should know that as \(n\) grows, this procedure becomes much more costly in terms of number of arithmetic operations involved than our inverse algorithm based on Gauss-Jordan elimination. You get a sense of this already from the previous \(3\times 3\) example. In general, the Gauss-Jordan inverse algorithm is the way to go.
