    Section 1.15 Subspaces

    We return now in earnest to our study of vector spaces. Our excursion in to the algebra matrices will been to be no passing dalliance. Indeed, not only will many examples of the concepts to come be drawn from the universe of matrices, matrices will also be used as tools to analyze these concepts. We will have plenty of opportunity to recite our solemn mantra.
    🔗
    In this section we will study subspaces, which are special subsets of vector spaces that respect the defining structure of a vector spaces: namely, the two vector operations. Definition 1.15.1 makes precise what we mean here by ‘respect’.
    🔗
    Subspaces arise naturally in any setting where vector spaces are at play, and are closely connected to solutions to linear systems. As we will see in Theorem 1.15.5, subspaces of vector spaces are vector spaces in their own right, furnishing us with yet more interesting examples of vector spaces.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.15.1 Definition and examples

    Definition 1.15.1. Subspace.

    Let \(V\) be an \(F\)-vector space. A subset \(W\subseteq V\) is a subspace of \(V\) if the following conditions hold.
    1. \(W\) contains the zero vector.
      \(\boldzero\in W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(W\) is closed under addition.
      For all \(v_1, v_2\in V\text{,}\) if \(v_1, v_2\in W\text{,}\) then \(v_1+ v_2\in W\text{.}\) Using logical shorthand:
      \begin{equation*} v_1, v_2\in W\implies v_1+ v_2\in W\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(W\) is closed under scalar multiplication.
      For all \(c\in F\) and \(v\in V\text{,}\) if \(v\in W\text{,}\) then \(c v\in W\text{.}\) Using logical shorthand:
      \begin{equation*} v\in W\Rightarrow c v\in W\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.15.2. Trivial subspaces.

    Let \(V\) be an \(F\)-vector space. The following are subspaces of \(V\text{:}\)
    1. \(\displaystyle W_1=\{\boldzero\}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle W_2=V\)
      🔗
      🔗
    In other words, the set containing just the zero vector is a subspace of \(V\text{,}\) as is \(V\) itself. These are sometimes called the trivial subspaces of \(V\text{.}\) Observe that every subspace \(W\) lies between these two: i.e., we have \(\{\boldzero\}\subseteq W\subseteq V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We prove that \(W_1=\{\boldzero\}\) is a subspace of \(V\text{,}\) leaving the proof for \(W_2=V\) to the reader.
    1. By definition, \(\boldzero\in W_1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Since \(\boldzero\) is the only element of \(W_1\) and since \(\boldzero+\boldzero=\boldzero\in W_1\text{,}\) we see that \(W_1\) is closed under addition.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Since \(\boldzero\) is the only element of \(W_1\text{,}\) we need only show that \(c\boldzero\in W_1\) for all scalars \(c\in F\text{.}\) But we have \(c\boldzero=\boldzero\) by Proposition 1.4.15. Thus \(c\boldzero\in W_1\text{,}\) as desired.
      🔗
      🔗
    This proves \(W_1=\{\boldzero\}\) is a subspace of \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.15.3.

    Let \(V=\R^2\) and let
    \begin{equation*} W=\{(t,t)\in\R^2 \colon t\in\R\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Prove that \(W\) is a subspace.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We must show properties (i)-(iii) hold for \(W\text{.}\)
    1. The zero element of \(V\) is \(\boldzero=(0,0)\text{,}\) which is certainly of the form \((t,t)\text{.}\) Thus \(\boldzero\in W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We must prove the implication \(v_1, v_2\in W\Rightarrow v_1+ v_2\in W\text{.}\)
      \begin{align*} v_1, v_2\in W\amp \Rightarrow\amp v_1=(t,t), v_2=(s,s) \text{ for some \(t,s\in\R\) }\\ \amp \Rightarrow\amp v_1+ v_2=(t+s,t+s)\\ \amp \Rightarrow\amp v_1+ v_2\in W\text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. We must prove the implication \(v\in W\Rightarrow c v\in W\text{,}\) for any \(c\in \R\text{.}\) We have
      \begin{align*} v\in W\amp \Rightarrow\amp v=(t,t)\\ \amp \Rightarrow\amp c v=(ct,ct)\\ \amp \Rightarrow\amp c v\in W \end{align*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.15.4.

    Let \(V=\R^2\) and let
    \begin{equation*} W=\{(x,y)\in \R^2\colon x, y\geq 0\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Is \(W\) a vector space? Decide which of the of properties (i)-(iii) in Definition 1.15.1 (if any) are satisfied by \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Clearly \(\boldzero=(0,0)\in W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose \(v_1=(x_1,y_1), v_2=(x_2,y_2)\in W\text{.}\) Then \(x_1, x_2, y_1, y_2\geq 0\text{,}\) in which case \(x_1+x_2, y_1+y_2\geq 0\text{,}\) and hence \(v_1+ v_2\in W\text{.}\) Thus \(W\) is closed under addition.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The set \(W\) is not closed under scalar multiplication. Indeed, let \(v=(1,1)\in W\text{.}\) Then \((-2) v=(-2,-2)\notin W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    As we now endeavor to show, if \(W\) is a subspace of a vector space \(V\text{,}\) then it inherits a vector space structure from \(V\) by simply restricting the vector operations defined on \(V\) to the subset \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 1.15.5. Subspaces are vector spaces.

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of the vector space \(V\text{.}\)
    1. The vector operations of \(V\) restrict to operations on \(W\) that satisfy the vector space axioms.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The zero vector of \(W\text{,}\) considered as a vector space, is the zero vector of \(V\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Given an element \(\boldw\in W\text{,}\) its vector inverse with respect to the vector space structure of \(W\) is equal to its vector inverse with respect to the vector space structure of \(V\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    It is important to understand how Axioms i–iii of Definition 1.15.1 come into play here. Without them we would not be able to say that restricting the vector operations of \(V\) to elements of \(W\) actually gives rise to well-defined operations on \(W\text{.}\) To be well-defined the operations must output elements that lie not just in \(V\text{,}\) but in \(W\) itself. This is precisely what being closed under addition and scalar multiplication guarantees. In more detail, since \(\boldw_1+\boldw_2\in W\) for all \(w_1,w_2\in W\) (Axiom (ii)), the vector addition on \(V\) gives rise by restriction to a well-defined operation on \(W\text{;}\) similarly, since \(c\boldw\in W\) for all \(c\in \R\) and \(\boldw\in W\text{,}\) the scalar multiplication operation on \(V\) gives rise by restriction to a well-defined scalar multiplication on \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Once we know restriction gives rise to well-defined operations on \(W\text{,}\) verifying the \(arithmetic axioms\) of Definition 1.4.2 amounts simply to observing that if a condition is true for all \(v\) in \(V\text{,}\) it is certainly true for all \(v\) in the subset \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The “existential axioms” (iii) and (iv) of Definition 1.4.2, however, require special consideration. By definition, a subspace \(W\) contains the zero vector of \(V\text{,}\) and clearly this still acts as the zero vector when we restrict the vector operations to \(W\text{.}\) What about vector inverses? We know that for any \(v\in W\) there is a vector inverse \(- v\) lying somewhere in \(V\text{.}\) We must show that in fact \(- v\) lies in \(W\text{:}\) i.e. we need to show that the operation of taking the vector inverse is well-defined on \(W\text{.}\) We prove this as follows:
    \begin{align*} v\in W \amp\implies (-1) v\in W \amp \\ \amp\implies -v\in W \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/prop_vs_props.html}{\text{1.4.15}}) \text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    It is important to understand how Axioms i–iii of Definition 1.15.1 come into play here. Without them we would not be able to say that restricting the vector operations of \(V\) to elements of \(W\) actually gives rise to well-defined operations on \(W\text{.}\) To be well-defined the operations must output elements that lie not just in \(V\text{,}\) but in \(W\) itself. This is precisely what being closed under addition and scalar multiplication guarantees.
    🔗
    Once we know restriction gives rise to well-defined operations on \(W\text{,}\) verifying the axioms of Definition 1.4.2 mostly amounts to observing that if a condition is true for all \(\boldv\) in \(V\text{,}\) it is certainly true for all \(\boldv\) in the subset \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The “existential axioms” (iii) and (iv) of Definition 1.4.2, however, require special consideration. By definition, a subspace \(W\) contains the zero vector of \(V\text{,}\) and clearly this still acts as the zero vector when we restrict the vector operations to \(W\text{.}\) What about vector inverses? We know that for any \(\boldv\in W\) there is a vector inverse \(-\boldv\) lying somewhere in \(V\text{.}\) We must show that in fact \(-\boldv\) lies in \(W\text{:}\) i.e. we need to show that the operation of taking the vector inverse is well-defined on \(W\text{.}\) We prove this as follows:
    \begin{align*} \boldv\in W \amp\implies (-1)\boldv\in W \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/d_subspace.html}{\text{1.15.1}}, \text{(iii) } )\\ \amp\implies -\boldv\in W \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/prop_vs_props.html}{\text{1.4.15}}, (iii)) \text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    We now know how to determine whether a given subset of a vector space is in fact a subspace. We are also interested in means of constructing subspaces from some given ingredients. The result below tells us that taking the intersection of a given collection of subspaces results in a subspace.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.15.6. Intersection of subspaces.

    Let \(V\) be an \(F\)-vector space. Given a tuple \((W_i)_{i\in I}\text{,}\) where \(W_i\) is a subspace of \(V\) for all \(i\in I\text{,}\) the intersection
    \begin{equation*} \bigcap_{i\in I}W_i=\{v\in V\mid v_i\in W_i \text{ for all } i\in I\} \end{equation*}
    is a subspace of \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Exercise.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.15.7. Unions of subspaces.

    While the intersection of subspaces is again a subspace, the same is not true for unions of subspaces.
    🔗
    For example, take \(V=\R^2\text{,}\) \(W_1=\{(t,t)\colon t\in\R\}\) and \(W_2=\{(t,-t)\colon t\in\R\}\text{.}\) Then each \(W_i\) is a subspace, but their union \(W_1\cup W_2\) is not.
    🔗
    Indeed, observe that \(\boldw_1=(1,1)\in W_1\subset W_1\cup W_2\) and \(\boldw_2=(1,-1)\in W_2\subset W_1\cup W_2\text{,}\) but \(\boldw_1+\boldw_2=(2,0)\notin W_1\cup W_2\text{.}\) Thus \(W_1\cup W_2\) is not closed under addition. (Interestingly, it is closed under scalar multiplication.)
    🔗
    🔗
    Before moving on to more examples of subspaces, we provide a procedure for determining whether something is a subspace. Note that the procedure merges the verification of Axioms (ii)-(iii) of Definition 1.15.1 into a single step.
    🔗

    Procedure 1.15.8. Two-step proof for subspaces.

    We can merge conditions (ii)-(iii) of Definition 1.15.1 into a single statement about linear combinations, deriving the following two-step method for proving that a set \(W\) is a subspace of a vector space \(V\text{.}\)
    1. Show \(\boldzero\in W\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Show that
      \begin{equation*} v_1, v_2\in W\implies c v_1+d v_2\in W\text{,} \end{equation*}
      for all \(c,d\in F\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    As a first illustration of the two-step technique, we show that the span of a set of vectors is always a subspace.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.15.9. Spans are subspaces.

    Let \(S\) be a subset of the \(F\)-vector space \(V\text{.}\)
    1. The set \(\Span S\) is a subspace of \(V\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(W\) is any subspace containing \(S\text{,}\) then \(\Span S\subseteq W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Taken together, (1) and (2) imply that \(\Span S\) is the smallest subspace of \(V\) containing \(S\).
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We prove each statement separately.
    🔗
    Statement (1).
    To show \(\Span S\) is a subspace, we use the two-step technique.
    1. By Remark 1.6.2 we know that \(\boldzero\in \Span S \text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose \(\boldv, \boldw\in S\text{.}\) By definition we have
      \begin{align*} \boldv \amp =c_1\boldv_1+c_2\boldv_2+\cdots +c_r\boldv_r \amp \boldw \amp = c_{r+1}\boldv_{r+1}+c_{r+2}\boldv_{r+2}+\cdots +c_{r+s}\boldv_{r+s} \end{align*}
      for some vectors \(\boldv_1, \boldv_2, \dots, \boldv_{r+s}\in S\) and scalars \(c_1,c_2,\dots, c_{r+s}\text{.}\) Then for any \(c,d\in \R\) we have
      \begin{equation*} c\boldv+d\boldw=cc_1\boldv_1+cc_2\boldv_2+\cdots +cc_r\boldv_r+dc_{r+1}\boldv_{r+1}+dc_{r+2}\boldv_{r+2}+\cdots +dc_{r+s}\boldv_{r+s}\text{,} \end{equation*}
      which is clearly a linear combination of elements of \(S\text{.}\) Thus \(c\boldv+d\boldw\in \Span S\text{,}\) as desired.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Statement (2).
    Let \(W\subseteq V\) be a subspace that contains all elements of \(S\text{.}\) Since \(W\) is closed under arbitrary linear combinations, it must contain any linear combination of elements of \(S\text{,}\) and thus \(\Span S\subseteq W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.15.2 Subspaces of \(F^n\)

    Theorem 1.15.11 gives a convenient method of producing a subspace \(W\) of \(F^n\text{:}\) namely, given any \(A\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\) , the set
    \begin{equation*} W=\{\boldx\in F^n\colon A\boldx=\boldzero\} \end{equation*}
    of all solutions to the homogeneous linear system \(A\boldx=\boldzero\) is guaranteed to be a subspace of \(F^n\text{.}\) We call this set the null space of the matrix \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 1.15.10. Null space of matrix.

    Let \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\text{.}\) The null space of \(A\text{,}\) denoted \(\NS A\text{,}\) is the set of all solutions to the matrix equation
    \begin{equation} A\boldx=\underset{m\times 1}{\boldzero}\text{.}\tag{1.15.1} \end{equation}
    In other words,
    \begin{equation*} \NS A=\{\boldx\in F^n\colon A\boldx=\boldzero\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Equivalently, thinking in terms of linear systems, \(\NS A\) is the set of solutions to the homogeneous linear system represented by (1.15.1).
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.15.11. Null spaces of matrices.

    Given any \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\text{,}\) its null space
    \begin{equation*} \NS A=\{\boldx\in F^n\colon A\boldx=\boldzero\} \end{equation*}
    is a subspace of \(F^n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Following the two-step technique, we first show that the zero vector \(\boldzero=(0,0,\dots, 0)\) of \(F^n\) lies in \(\NS A\text{.}\) This is clear, since
    \begin{equation*} A\underset{n\times 1}{\boldzero}=\underset{m\times 1}{\boldzero}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Next, we show that for any \(\boldx_1, \boldx_2\in F^n\) and any \(c_1, c_2\in F\) we have
    \begin{equation*} \boldx_1, \boldx_2\in \NS A\implies c_1\boldw_1+c_2\boldw_2\in \NS A\text{.} \end{equation*}
    If \(\boldx_1, \boldx_2\in \NS A\text{,}\) then we have \(A\boldx_1=A\boldx_2=\boldzero\text{,}\) by definition. It then follows that the vector \(c_1\boldx_1+c_2\boldx_2\) satisfies
    \begin{align*} A(c_1\boldx_1+c_2\boldx_2) \amp= c_1A\boldx_1+c_2A\boldx_2 \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/th_matrix_alg_props.html}{\text{1.10.1}}) \\ \amp c_1A\boldzero_m+c_2\boldzero_m \amp (\boldx_1, \boldx_2\in W) \\ \amp = \boldzero\text{.} \end{align*}
    Since \(A(c\boldx_1+d\boldx_2)=\boldzero\text{,}\) we have \(c_1\boldx_1+c_2\boldx_2\in \NS A\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.15.12. Alternative subspace method.

    Theorem 1.15.11 provides an alternative way of showing that a subset \(W\subseteq \R^n\text{:}\) namely, find an \(m\times n\) matrix \(A\) for which we have \(W=\{\boldx\in \R^n\colon A\boldx=\boldzero\}\text{.}\) This is often much faster than using the two-step technique.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.15.13. Subspace as null space.

    Define the subset \(W\) of \(\R^3\) as
    \begin{equation*} W=\{(x,y,z)\in \R^3\colon x+2y+3z=x-y-z=0\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    1. Prove that \(W\) is a subspace by identifying it as the set of solutions to a homogeneous matrix equation.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Use (a) and Gaussian elimination to compute a parametric description of \(W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. It is easy to see that
      \begin{equation*} W=\{\boldx\in \R^n\colon A\boldx=\boldzero\} \end{equation*}
      where
      \begin{equation*} A=\begin{amatrix}[rrr]1\amp 2\amp 3\\ 1\amp -1\amp -1 \end{amatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      We conclude \(W\) is a subspace.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The augmented matrix \(\begin{amatrix}[c|c]A\amp\boldzero \end{amatrix}\) row reduces to
      \begin{equation*} U=\begin{amatrix}[rrr|r]\boxed{1}\amp 2\amp 3\amp 0\\ 0 \amp \boxed{1}\amp 1\amp 0 \end{amatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Following Procedure 1.8.21 we conclude that
      \begin{equation*} W=\{(-t,-2t,t)\colon t\in \R\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Geometrically this is the line in \(\R^3\) passing through \((0,0,0)\) with direction vector \((1,2,-1)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

    Warning 1.15.14. Subspace as null space.

    As convenient as the method described in Remark 1.15.12 and illustrated in Example 1.15.13 may be, bear in mind that it cannot always be used. Indeed, by definition the null space of an \(m\times n\) matrix is a subset of \(F^n\text{.}\) Thus this method can only be employed when the ambient vector space is \(F^n\text{.}\) Don’t forget that there are other vector spaces besides \(F^n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Let \(A\) be an \(m\times n\) matrix. If \(\boldb\in F^m\) is nonzero, then the set of solutions \(S\) to \(A\boldx=\boldb\) is not a subspace of \(F^n\text{,}\) and for a very simple reason: since
    \begin{equation*} A\underset{n\times 1}{\boldzero}=\underset{m\times 1}{\boldzero}\ne \boldb\text{,} \end{equation*}
    we see that \(\underset{n\times 1}{\boldzero}\notin S\text{,}\) and thus \(S\) is not a subspace. Thus, thinking in terms of linear systems, we see that while the set of solutions to a homogenous linear system constitutes a subspace, the set of solutions to a nonhomogeneous system does not. On the other hand, as articulated by Theorem 1.15.15, the set of solutions to a nonhomogeneous linear system can be thought of as a translate of a vector space.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.15.15. Null space and linear systems.

    Let \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\) and \(\boldb\in F^m\text{,}\) and let \(S\) be the set of all solutions to the linear system
    \begin{equation} A\boldx=\boldb\text{.}\tag{1.15.2} \end{equation}
    1. \(S\) is a subspace of \(F^n\) if and only if \(\boldb=\boldzero\text{:}\) i.e., if and only if the linear system (1.15.2) is homogeneous.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(\boldx_p\) is a solution to (1.15.2), then we have
      \begin{equation*} S=\boldx_p+\NS A=\{\boldx_p+\boldw\colon \boldw\in \NS A\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      In other words, given a particular solution \(\boldx_p\) to (1.15.2), the general solution is given by
      \begin{equation*} \boldx_p+\boldx_h \end{equation*}
      where \(\boldx_h\in \NS A\) is a solution to the homogeneous linear system
      \begin{equation} A\boldx=\boldzero\text{.}\tag{1.15.3} \end{equation}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    1. If \(\boldb=\boldzero\text{,}\) then \(S=\NS A\text{,}\) and this is a subspace by Theorem 1.15.11. If \(\boldb\ne \boldzero\text{,}\) then \(\boldzero\notin S\text{,}\) and hence \(S\) is not a subspace.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Let \(\boldx_p\) satisfy (1.15.2). We show that
      \begin{equation*} S=\boldx_p+\NS A \end{equation*}
      by showing the two inclusions
      \begin{align*} \boldx_p+\NS A\amp \subseteq S \amp S\amp \boldx_p+\NS A\text{.} \end{align*}
      If \(\boldv\in \boldx_p+\NS A\text{,}\) then we have \(\boldv=\boldx_p+\boldw\) for some \(\boldw\in \NS A\text{,}\) in which case
      \begin{align*} A\boldv \amp = A(\boldx_p+\boldw)\\ \amp = A\boldx_p+A\boldw\\ \amp = \boldb+\boldzero \amp (\boldx_p\in S, \boldw\in \NS A)\\ \amp = \boldb\text{.} \end{align*}
      This shows that if \(\boldv\in \boldx_p+\NS A\text{,}\) then \(\boldv\in S\text{,}\) and thus that \(\boldx_p+A\subseteq S\text{.}\) For the other inclusion, if \(\boldv\in S\text{,}\) then we have
      \begin{align*} A(\boldx_p-\boldv) \amp = A\boldx_p-A\boldv\\ \amp = \boldb-\boldb\\ \amp = \boldzero\text{,} \end{align*}
      showing that \((\boldx_p-\boldv)\in \NS A\text{.}\) But then we have
      \begin{equation*} \boldv=\boldx_p+\underset{\boldw}{(\boldx_p-\boldv)}\text{,} \end{equation*}
      where \(\boldw=(\boldx_p-\boldv)\in \NS A\text{.}\) Thus \(\boldv\in \boldx_p+\NS A\text{,}\) showing that \(S\subseteq \boldx_p+\NS A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Corollary 1.15.16. Null space and linear systems.

    Let \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\) and \(\boldb\in F^m\text{,}\) and suppose the linear system \(A\boldx=\boldb\) is consistent.
    1. There is a unique solution to the system if and only if \(\NS A=\{\boldzero\}\text{:}\) i.e., if and only if the only solution to \(A\boldx=\boldzero\) is the trivial one \(\boldx=\boldzero\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. There are infinitely many solutions if and only if there is a nonzero solution to \(A\boldx=\boldzero\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.15.17. Hyperplane.

    A linear equation
    \begin{equation} a_1x_1+a_2x_2+\cdots a_nx_n=b\tag{1.15.5} \end{equation}
    over a field \(F\) is nontrivial if \(a_i\ne 0\) for some \(1\leq i\leq n\text{.}\) Given a nontrivial equation (1.15.5), the set of solutions
    \begin{equation*} S=\{(s_1,s_2,\dots, s_n)\in F^n\colon a_1s_1+\cdots+a_ns_n=b \} \end{equation*}
    is called a hyperplane in \(F^n\text{.}\) We have the two following special cases, corresponding to \(n=2\) and \(n=3\text{:}\)
    • A hyperplane in \(F^2\) is the set of solutions to a nontrivial equation \(ax+by=c\text{,}\) and is called a line.
      🔗
      🔗
    • A hyperplane in \(F^3\) is the set of solutions to a nontrivial equation \(ax+by+cz=d\text{,}\) and is called a plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.15.18. Hyperplanes and subspaces.

    Let \(H\subseteq F^n\) be the hyperplane with defining equation
    \begin{equation} a_1x_1+a_2x_2+\cdots +a_nx_n=b\text{.}\tag{1.15.6} \end{equation}
    In terms of Theorem 1.15.15, \(H\) is just the set of solutions to the matrix equation \(A\boldx=\boldb\text{,}\) where
    \begin{align*} A \amp =\begin{bmatrix} a_1\amp a_2\amp \dots \amp a_n \end{bmatrix} \amp \boldb=\begin{bmatrix} b \end{bmatrix} \text{.} \end{align*}
    It follows from Theorem 1.15.15 that \(H\) is a subspace if and only if \(b=0\text{:}\) i.e., if and only if \(H\) passes through the origin. Furthermore, if \(b\ne 0\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} H=\boldx_p+H_0\text{,} \end{equation*}
    where \(\boldx_p\) is any solution to (1.15.6), and \(H_0\) is the set of solutions to the corresponding homogeneous equation
    \begin{equation} a_1x_1+a_2x_2+\cdots +a_nx_n=0\text{.}\tag{1.15.7} \end{equation}
    In other words, although it is not true in general that every hyperplane \(H\subseteq F^n\) is a subspace (since it may not pass through the origin), it is true that \(H=\boldx_p+H_0\) is a translate of a hyperplane \(H_0\) that is a subspace (since \(H_0\) passes through the origin).
    🔗
    🔗
    In addition to the null space, there are a number of other subspaces of that we naturally associate to a matrix \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\text{,}\) and which make use of the span oepration: namely, the the row space and column space of the matrix. Taken together, these three subspaces associated to \(A\) are called its fundamental spaces.
    🔗

    Definition 1.15.19. Fundamental spaces.

    Let \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\text{,}\) let \(\boldr_1,\dots, \boldr_m\in F^n\) be the \(m\) rows of \(A\text{,}\) and let \(\boldc_1,\dots \boldc_n\in F^m\) be its \(n\) columns. The following subspaces are called the fundamental subspaces of \(A\).
    • Null space.
      The null space of \(A\), denoted \(\NS A\) is defined as
      \begin{equation*} \NS A =\{\boldx\in F^n\colon A\boldx=\boldzero\}\subseteq F^n.\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • Row space.
      The row space of \(A\), denoted \(\RS A\text{,}\) is defined as
      \begin{equation*} \RS A=\Span \{\boldr_1, \boldr_2, \dots, \boldr_m\}\subseteq F^n\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • Column space.
      The column space of \(A\), denoted \(\CS A\text{,}\) is defined as
      \begin{equation*} \CS A=\Span \{\boldc_1, \boldc_2, \dots, \boldc_n\}\subseteq F^m\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.15.20. Fundamental spaces.

    Note that the fundamental spaces of a matrix \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\) are indeed subspaces. This is a simple consequences of theorems 1.15.11 and 1.15.9, which tell us that null spaces of matrices and spans of vectors are subspaces.
    🔗
    A good practice when dealing with fundamental spaces of a matrix, is to first sort out the ambient space where each of these subspaces lives: if \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\text{,}\) then both \(\NS A\) and \(\RS A\) are subspaces of \(F^n\text{,}\) and \(\CS A\) is a subspace of \(F^m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.15.21. Fundamental spaces: elementary example.

    Provide bases and compute the dimension of the three fundamental spaces of
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{amatrix}[rrr] 1 \amp 2\amp 3 \\ 1 \amp 2\amp 3 \end{amatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    By definition, we have
    \begin{align*} \NS A=\{\boldx\in \R^3\colon A\boldx=\boldzero\}\amp \subseteq \R^3 \amp \\ \RS A=\Span\{(1,2,3),(1,2,3)\} \amp\subseteq \R^3 \\ \CS A=\Span\{(1,1),(2,2),(3,3)\}\amp \subseteq \R^2 \text{.} \end{align*}
    We see easily by inspection that
    \begin{equation*} \RS A=\Span\{(1,2,3),(1,2,3)\}=\Span\{(1,2,3)\}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    a line in \(\R^3\) passing through the origin, and
    \begin{equation*} \CS A=\Span\{(1,1),(2,2),(3,3)\}=\Span\{(1,1)\}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    a line in \(\R^2\) passing through the origin. Since the sets \(B_1=\{(1,2,3)\}\) and \(B_2=\{(1,1)\}\) are each linearly independent, they are clearly bases for their spans \(\RS A \) and \(\CS A\text{,}\) respectively. It follows that \(\dim \RS A=\dim \CS A=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Next, by definition \(\NS A\) is the set of vectors \(\boldx=(x_1,x_2,x_3)\in \R^3\) satisfying
    \begin{equation*} A\boldx=\begin{bmatrix} x_1+2x_2+3x_3\\ x_1+2x_2+3x_3 \end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    or equivalently, the solutions to the linear equation \(x_1+2x_2+3x_3=0 \text{,}\) a plane in \(\R^3\) passing through the origin. Using Procedure 1.8.21 we derive the parametric description
    \begin{align*} \NS A\amp=\{(-(2s+3t),s,t)\colon s,t\in \R\}\\ \amp =\{s(-2,1,0),+t(-3,0,1)\colon s,t\in \R\} \text{,} \end{align*}
    from which we see that
    \begin{equation*} \NS A=\Span\{(-2,1,0),(-3,0,1)\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Since \(B_3=\{(-2,1,0),(-3,0,1)\}\) is linearly independent and spans \(\NS A\text{,}\) it is a basis for \(\NS A\text{.}\) We conclude \(\dim \NS A=2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    The null space of a matrix \(A\) has an obvious connection with systems of linear equations, it being the set of solutions to the homogeneous linear system represented by \(A\boldx=\boldzero\text{.}\) The next theorem provides an interpretation of the column space of \(A\) in terms of linear systems.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.15.23. Column space and linear systems.

    Let \(A\) be an \(m\times n\) matrix, and let \(\boldb\in \R^m\text{.}\) The following statements are equivalent.
    1. \(\boldb\in \CS A\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. There exists \(\boldx\in \R^n\) such that \(\boldb=A\boldx\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The linear system \(A\boldx=\boldb\) is consistent.
      🔗
      🔗
    As a consequence, we have xre
    \begin{align} \CS A \amp =\{\boldb\in \R^m \colon \boldb=A\boldx \text{ for some } \boldx\in \R^n\}\tag{1.15.8}\\ \amp = \{\boldb\in \R^m\colon \text{ the linear system } A\boldx=\boldb \text{ is consistent}\}\text{.}\tag{1.15.9} \end{align}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Statements (2) and (3) are equivalent by virtue of the definition of a consistent system. Statements (1) and (2) are equivalent thanks to the column method of matrix multiplication. Indeed, letting \(\bolda_1, \bolda_2,\dots, \bolda_n\in \R^m\) be the columns of \(A\text{,}\) so that
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{bmatrix} \vert \amp \vert \amp \amp \vert \\ \bolda_1\amp \bolda_2\amp \cdots \amp \bolda_n\\ \vert \amp \vert \amp \amp \vert \end{bmatrix}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    we have
    \begin{align*} \boldb\in \CS A \amp \iff \boldb=c_1\bolda_1+c_2\bolda_2+\cdots +c_n\bolda_n \text{ for some } c_i\in \R \amp (\text{def.})\\ \amp \iff \boldb=A\boldx, \text{ where } \boldx=(c_1,c_2,\dots, c_n)\in \R^n \amp \knowl{./knowl/xref/th_column_method.html}{\text{1.9.12}}\text{.} \end{align*}
    The set equalities (1.15.8)–(1.15.9) follow immediately from the equivalence of statements (1)-(3).

    Remark 1.15.24. What about the row space?

    You might be wondering why we give the row space of a matrix such short shrift here. As it turns out, the null space and column space of a matrix will be of the most importance to us algorithmically, with row space playing more of a supporting role of convenience. That said, as we will be able to show once we know more about inner products, the row space of a matrix \(A\) also has a connection with linear systems associated to \(A\text{:}\) namely, it is the orthogonal complement of the null space of \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    For more matrices more complicated than the one in Example 1.15.21, the key to computing the various fundamental spaces lies with Gaussian elimination. Our first theorem lays out how exactly this procedure affects the fundamental spaces of a matrix.
    🔗
    The next theorem indicates how row reduction affects fundamental spaces.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.15.25. Fundamental spaces and row operations.

    Let \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\text{,}\) and suppose \(A\) is row equivalent to \(B\text{.}\)
    1. \(\NS A=\NS B\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\RS A=\RS B\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. In general \(\CS A \) is not equal to \(\CS B\text{.}\) However, letting \(\bolda_i, \boldb_i\in F^m\) be the columns of \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) respectively, the columns \(\bolda_{i_1}, \bolda_{i_2},\dots, \bolda_{i_r}\) form span \(\CS A\) if and only if the columns \(\boldb_{i_1}, \boldb_{i_2},\dots, \boldb_{i_r}\) span \(\CS B\text{.}\)

    Remark 1.15.26. Column space and row reduction.

    Suppose the matrices \(A\) and \(B\) are row equivalent. As indicated in Theorem 1.15.25 we typically have \(\CS A\ne \CS B\text{.}\) It is useful to have simple examples of this phenomenon at the ready. Example 1.15.27 provides one such example.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.15.27. Column space and row reduction.

    The matrix
    \begin{align*} A \amp = \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\\ 1\amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{align*}
    row reduces to the matrix
    \begin{align*} U \amp = \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\\ 0 \amp 0\end{bmatrix} \end{align*}
    in row echelon form. By inspection we see that
    \begin{align*} \CS A \amp =\Span\{(1,1)\}=\{(t,t)\colon t\in \R\}\\ \CS U \amp =\Span\{(1,0)\}=\{(t,0)\colon t\in \R\}\text{.} \end{align*}
    It is clear algebraically from the two set descriptions that \(\CS A\ne \CS U\text{.}\) Geometrically, the two spaces are two distinct lines in \(\R^2\) passing through the origin: \(\CS A\) is the line defined by the equation \(y=x\) and \(\CS U\) is the \(x\)-axis.
    🔗
    By contrast consider the matrix
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\\ 0\amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    which is row equivalent to
    \begin{equation*} U=\begin{bmatrix} 1\amp 0 \\ 0\amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \text{.} \end{equation*}
    In this case we have
    \begin{align*} \CS A \amp =\Span\{(1,0),(1,1)\}=\R^2\\ \CS U \amp =\Span\{(1,0),(0,1)\}=\R^2\text{,} \end{align*}
    and thus \(\CS A=\CS U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Using Theorem 1.15.25, to find bases of the fundamental spaces of a matrix, we can first row reduce to a matrix \(U\) in row echelon form. The question remains, how best to describe these spaces for \(U\) in an efficient manner. We will have a better answer for that once we have introduced the notions of bases and dimension.
