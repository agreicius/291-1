    Section 1.20 Null space and image

    Having introduced linear transformations, we now begin to investigate them as important mathematical objects in their own right. In this section we introduce two subspaces associated to a linear transformation \(T\colon V\rightarrow W\) that will aid this investigation. These are the null space and image of a transformation.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.20.1 Definition and examples

    Definition 1.20.1 Null space and image.

    Let \(T\colon V\rightarrow W\) be a linear transformation.
    • Null space of \(T\).
      The null space of \(T\) is the subset \(\NS T\) of \(V\) defined as
      \begin{equation} \NS T=\{v\in V\colon T(v)=\boldzero\}\text{.}\tag{1.20.1} \end{equation}
      🔗
      🔗
    • Image of \(T\).
      The image (or range) of \(T\) is the subset \(\im T\) of \(W\) defined as
      \begin{equation} \im T=\{w\in W\colon w=T(v) \text{ for some } v\in V\}\text{.}\tag{1.20.2} \end{equation}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.20.2 Null space and image.

    We gather a few simple observations about the null space and image of a linear map \(T\colon V\rightarrow W\)
    1. Let \(T\colon V\rightarrow W\text{.}\) It is useful to keep in mind where \(\NS T\) and \(\im T\) “live” in this picture: we have \(\NS T\subseteq V\) and \(\im T\subseteq W\text{.}\) In other words, the null space is a subset of the domain, and the image is a subset of the codomain. See Figure 1.20.3.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Note that the image \(\im T\) of a linear transformation is just its image when considered simply as a function of sets. (See Definition 0.2.6.)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The notion of a null space is analogous to the set of zeros (or roots) of a real-valued function \(f\colon X\rightarrow \R\text{,}\)
      \begin{equation*} \{x\in X\colon f(x)=0\}\text{,} \end{equation*}
      and “the zeros of \(T\)” is a useful English shorthand for \(\NS T\text{.}\) However, there is an important difference between the null space of a linear transformation and the zeros of an arbitrary real-valued function: the null space of a linear transformation comes with the added structure of a vector space (Theorem 1.20.4), whereas the zeros of an arbitrary function in general do not.
      🔗
      The same observation can be made about the image of a linear transformation (Theorem 1.20.4), in comparison to the image of an arbitrary function.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Before getting to examples we make official what we hinted at above: the null space and image of a linear transformation are subspaces.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.20.4 Null space and image.

    If \(T\colon V\rightarrow W\) is a linear transformation, then \(\NS T\) is a subspace of \(V\text{,}\) and \(\im T\) is a subspace of \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Null space of \(T\).

    We use the two-step technique to prove \(\NS T\) is a subspace.
    1. Since \(T(\boldzero_V)=\boldzero_W\) (Theorem 1.19.9), we see that \(\boldzero_V\in \NS T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose \(\boldv_1, \boldv_2\in \NS T\text{.}\) Given any \(c,d\in \R\text{,}\) we have
      \begin{align*} T(c\boldv_1+d\boldv_2) \amp=cT(\boldv_1)+dT(\boldv_2) \amp (T \text{ is linear, } \knowl{./knowl/xref/th_transform_basic_props.html}{\text{Theorem 1.19.9}})\\ \amp=c\boldzero_W+d\boldzero_W \amp (\boldv_1, \boldv_2\in \NS T) \\ \amp = \boldzero_W\text{.} \end{align*}
      This shows that \(c\boldv_1+d\boldv_2\in \NS T\text{,}\) completing our proof.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Image of \(T\).

    The proof proceeds in a similar manner, using the two-step technique.
    1. Since \(T(\boldzero_V)=\boldzero_W\) (Theorem 1.19.9), we see that \(\boldzero_W\) is “hit” by \(T\text{,}\) and hence is a member of \(\im T\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Assume vectors \(\boldw_1, \boldw_2\in W\) are elements of \(\im T\text{.}\) By definition, this means there are vectors \(\boldv_1, \boldv_2\in V\) such that \(T(\boldv_i)=\boldw_i\) for \(1\leq i\leq 2\text{.}\) Now given any linear combination \(\boldw=c\boldw_1+d\boldw_2\text{,}\) we have
      \begin{align*} T(c\boldv_1+d\boldv_2) \amp = cT(\boldv_1)+dT(\boldv_2)\\ \amp =c\boldw_1+d\boldw_2\\ \amp =\boldw\text{.} \end{align*}
      This shows that for any linear combination \(\boldw=c\boldw_1+d\boldw_2\text{,}\) there is an element \(\boldv=c\boldv_1+d\boldv_2\) such that \(T(\boldv)=\boldw\text{.}\) We conclude that if \(\boldw_1, \boldw_2\in \im T\text{,}\) then \(\boldw=c\boldw_1+d\boldw_2\in \im T\) for any \(c,d\in \R\text{,}\) as desired.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.20.5.

    Define \(S\colon \ML_{n,n}(F)\rightarrow \ML_{n,n}(F)\) as \(S(A)=A^T-A\text{.}\)
    1. Prove that \(S\) is linear.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Identify \(\NS S\) as a familiar matrix subspace.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Identify \(\im S\) as a familiar matrix subspace.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Linearity is an easy consequence of transpose properties. For any \(A_1, A_2\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\) and \(c_1,c_2\in F\text{,}\) we have
      \begin{align*} S(c_1A_1+c_2A_2) \amp= (c_1A_1+c_2A_2)^T-(c_1A_1+c_2A_2) \\ \amp = c_1A_1^T+c_2A_2^T-c_1A_1-c_2A_2\amp (\text{transp. props}) \\ \amp =c_1(A_1^T-A_1)+c_2(A_2^T-A_2)\\ \amp =c_1S(A_1)+c_2S(A_2)\text{.} \end{align*}
      🔗
      Alternatively, since we already showed that the transpose operator
      \begin{align*} G\colon F^{nn} \amp \rightarrow F^{nn}\\ A \amp \mapsto A^T \end{align*}
      is linear on \(V=F^{nn}\text{,}\) and since \(S=G-I_V\text{,}\) it follows that \(G\) is linear.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We have
      \begin{align*} \NS S \amp= \{A\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\colon S(A)=\boldzero\} \\ \amp=\{A\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\colon A^T-A=\boldzero\} \\ \amp=\{A\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\colon A^T=A\} \text{.} \end{align*}
      Thus \(\NS F\) is the subspace of symmetric \(n\times n\) matrices!
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Let \(W=\{B\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\colon B^T=-B\}\text{,}\) subspace of skew-symmetric \(n\times n\) matrices. We claim \(\im S=W\text{.}\) As this is a set equality, we prove it by showing the two set inclusions \(\im S\subseteq W\) and \(W\subseteq \im S\text{.}\) (See Basic set properties)
      🔗
      The inclusion \(\im S\subseteq W\) is the easier of the two. If \(B\in \im S\text{,}\) then \(B=S(A)=A^T-A\) for some \(A\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\text{.}\) Using various properties of transposition, we have
      \begin{equation*} B^T=(A^T-A)^T=(A^T)^T-A^T=-(A^T-A)=-B\text{,} \end{equation*}
      showing that \(B\) is skew-symmetric, and thus \(B\in W\text{,}\) as desired.
      🔗
      The inclusion \(W\subseteq \im S\) is trickier: we must show that if \(B\) is skew-symmetric, then there is an \(A\) such that \(B=S(A)=A^T-A\text{.}\) Assume we have a \(B\) with \(B^T=-B\text{.}\) Letting \(A=-\frac{1}{2}B\) we have
      \begin{equation*} A^T-A=(-\frac{1}{2}B)^T+\frac{1}{2}B=\frac{1}{2}(-B^T+B)=\frac{1}{2}(B+B)=B\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Thus we have found a matrix \(A\) satisfying \(S(A)=B\text{.}\) It follows that \(B\in\im S\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.20.6 Differential operator.

    Let \(I=[a,b]\) be a closed finite interval of \(\R\text{.}\) Define \(T\colon C^1(I)\rightarrow C(I)\) as \(F(f)=f'\text{.}\)
    1. Identify \(\NS T\) as a familiar function subspace.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Identify \(\im T\) as a familiar function subspace.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. By definition, we have
      \begin{align*} \NS T \amp = \{f\in C^1([a,b])\colon f'=\boldzero\} \\ \amp = \{f\in C^1([a,b])\colon f'(x)=0 \text{ for all } x\in [a,b]\} \text{.} \end{align*}
      From calculus, we know that \(f'(x)=0\) for all \(x\in [a,b]\) if and only if \(f\) is a constant function. Thus $\NS T$ is the set of all constant functions on \([a,b]\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. By definition
      \begin{equation*} \im T=\{g\in C^1([a,b])\colon g=f' \text{ for some } f\in C^1([a,b])\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Equivalently, using some calculus terminology, \(\im T\) is the set of continuous functions \(g\) on \([a,b]\) that have an antiderivative. It follows from the fundamental theorem of calculus that in fact all continuous function on \([a,b]\) have an antiderivative. Thus \(\im T=C([a,b])\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.20.8 Polynomial evaluation.

    Show that \(W=\{f\in P_7(\C)\colon f(-1)=f(2i)=f(6)\}\) is a subspace of \(P_7(\C)\) using Procedure 1.20.7.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We make use of the evaluation linear transformations introduced earlier. Define the linear transformations
    \begin{align*} T\colon P_7(\C) \amp\rightarrow \C^2 \amp S\colon P_7(\C)\amp \rightarrow \C^2 \\ f \amp \longmapsto (f(-1),f(-1)) \amp f\amp \longmapsto (f(2i),f(6))\text{.} \end{align*}
    We see that \(f\in W\) if and only if \(T(f)=S(f)\text{,}\) or equivalently, if and only if \((T-S)(f)=\boldzero\text{.}\) Thus \(W=\NS(T-S)\text{,}\) the null space of the linear transformation \(T-S\text{.}\) It follows that \(W\) is a linear transformation.

    Example 1.20.9 Homogeneous linear differential equation.

    Let \(I\) be an interval of \(\R\text{,}\) let \(q_{n-1},q_{n-2},\dots, q_1\) be continuous functions on \(I\) (i.e., \(q_i\in C(I)\)) for all \(1\leq i\leq n-1\text{,}\) and define \(W\) as the set of functions \(f\in C^n(I)\) satisfying the linear homogeneous differential equation
    \begin{equation} f^{(n)}(x)+q_{n-1}(x)f^{(n-1)}(x)+\cdots +q_1(x)f(x)=0 \text{ for all } x\in I\text{.}\tag{1.20.3} \end{equation}
    Prove that \(W\) is a subspace of \(C^n(I)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Define \(T\colon C^n(I) \rightarrow C(I)\) as
    \begin{equation*} T(f)=f^{(n)}+q_{n-1}f^{(n-1)}+\cdots +q_1f\text{.} \end{equation*}
    We have
    \begin{align*} T(cf+dg) \amp = (f+g)^{(n)}+q_{n-1}(f+g)^{(n-1)}+\cdots +q_1(f+g)\\ \amp = c(f^{(n)}+q_{n-1}f^{(n-1)}+\cdots +q_1f)+d(g^{(n)}+q_{n-1}g^{(n-1)}+\cdots +q_1g)\\ \amp =cT(f)+dT(g)\text{,} \end{align*}
    showing that \(T\) is a linear transformation. The set of solutions to (1.20.3) is precisely \(\NS T\text{,}\) making this set a subspace of \(C^n(I)\text{.}\)

    Example 1.20.10 Matrix transformation.

    Let \(A\) be an \(m\times n\) matrix, and let \(\bolda_1,\bolda_2,\dots, \bolda_n\) be the \(n\) columns of \(A\text{,}\) considered as elements of \(F^m\text{.}\) Show that the null space and image of the corresponding matrix transformation \(T_A\colon F^n\rightarrow F^m\) can be identified with the following fundamental spaces of \(A\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \NS T_A \amp = \NS A=\{\boldx\in F^n\colon A\boldx=\boldzero\}\\ \im T_A \amp =\CS A=\Span(\bolda_1,\bolda_2,\dots, \bolda_n)\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    For the null space, we have
    \begin{align*} \NS T_A \amp =\{\boldx\in F^n\colon T_A(\boldx)=\boldzero\}\\ \amp = \{\boldx\in F^n\colon A\boldx=\boldzero\}\\ \amp = \NS A\text{.} \end{align*}
    The statment about \(\im T_A\) follows from the following observation about matrix equations: we can solve \(A\boldx=\boldy\) if and only if \(\boldy\) can be expressed as a linear combination of the columns \(\bolda_j\) of \(A\text{.}\) (This observation, in turn, follows from the column method description of matrix multiplication.) Taking this for granted, we then have
    \begin{align*} \im T_A \amp =\{\boldy\in F^m\colon y=T_A(\boldx) \text{ for some } \boldx\in F^n\}\\ \amp =\{\boldy\in F^m\colon y=A\boldx \text{ for some } \boldx\in F^n\}\\ \amp =\{\boldy\in F^m\colon y=\sum_{j=1}^nc_j\bolda_j \text{ for some } c_j\in F\}\\ \amp =\Span(\bolda_1,\bolda_2,\dots, \bolda_n)\\ \amp = \CS A\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    As with any function, one thing we wish to be able to determine about a linear transformation is whether it is injective, surjective, and bijective. The theorem below describes how \(\NS T\) and \(\im T\) answer these questions for us.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.20.11 Injective, surjective, bijective.

    Let \(T\colon V\rightarrow W\) be a linear transformation.
    1. \(T\) is injective if and only if \(\NS T=\{\boldzero\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(T\) is surjective if and only if \(\im T=W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(T\) is bijective if and only if \(\NS T=\{\boldzero\}\) and \(\im T=W\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. We prove both implications separately.
      🔗
      First observe that if \(T(v)=\boldzero\text{,}\) then \(T(v)=T(\boldzero)\text{,}\) since \(T(\boldzero)=\boldzero\text{.}\) Thus, if \(T\) is injective, then we have
      \begin{equation*} T(v)=\boldzero\implies T(v)=T(\boldzero)\implies v=\boldzero\text{.} \end{equation*}
      It follows that \(\NS T=\{\boldzero\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      Now assume that \(\NS T=\{\boldzero\}\text{.}\) Given \(v,v'\in V\text{,}\) we have
      \begin{align*} T(v)=T(v') \amp \implies T(v)-T(v')=\boldzero\\ \amp \implies T(v-v')=\boldzero \amp (T \text{ linear})\\ \amp \implies v-v'\in \NS T\\ \amp \implies v-v'=\boldzero \amp (\NS T=\{\boldzero\})\\ \amp \implies v=v'\text{.} \end{align*}
      We conclude that \(T\) is injective in this case.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. This is a purely set-theoretic result.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. This is an elementary consequence of (1) and (2).
      🔗
      🔗

    Subsection 1.20.2 Rank-nullity theorem

    We now prove the rank-nullity theorem, sometimes called the fundamental theorem of linear algebra. This theorem relates the dimensions of the null space and image of a linear transformation \(T\colon V\rightarrow W\text{,}\) assuming \(V\) is finite dimensional. Roughly speaking, it says that the bigger the null space, the smaller the image. More precisely, it tells us that
    \begin{equation*} \dim V=\dim\NS T+\dim\im T\text{.} \end{equation*}
    As we will see, this elegant result can be used to significantly simplify computations with linear transformations. For example, in a situation where we wish to compute explicitly both the null space and image of a given linear transformation, we can often get away with just computing one of the two spaces and using the rank-nullity theorem (and a dimension argument) to easily determine the other. Additionally, the rank-nullity theorem will directly imply some intuitively obvious properties of linear transformations. For example, suppose \(V\) is a finite-dimensional vector space. It seems obvious that if \(\dim W> \dim V\text{,}\) then there is no linear transformation mapping \(V\) surjectively onto \(W\text{:}\) i.e., you should not be able to map a “smaller” vector space onto a “bigger” one. Similarly, if \(\dim W \lt \dim V\text{,}\) then we expect that there is no injective linear transformation mapping \(V\) injectively into \(W\text{.}\) Both these results are easy consequences of the rank-nullity theorem.
    🔗
    Before proving the theorem we give names to \(\dim \NS T\) and \(\dim\im T\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 1.20.12 Rank and nullity.

    Let \(T\colon V\rightarrow W\) be a linear transformation.
    • The rank of \(T\text{,}\) denoted \(\rank T\text{,}\) is the dimension of \(\im T\text{:}\) i.e.,
      \begin{equation*} \rank T=\dim\im T\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • The nullity of \(T\text{,}\) denoted \(\nullity T\text{,}\) is the dimension of \(\NS T\text{:}\) i.e.,
      \begin{equation*} \nullity T=\dim\NS T\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.20.13 Rank-nullity theorem.

    Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space. If \(T\colon V\rightarrow W\) is a linear transformation, then
    \begin{equation} \dim V=\dim\NS T+\dim \im T=\nullity T+\rank T\text{.}\tag{1.20.4} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Choose a basis \(B'=(\boldv_1, \boldv_2, \dots, \boldv_k)\) of \(\NS T\) and extend \(B'\) to a basis \(B=(\boldv_1, \boldv_2,\dots, \boldv_k,\boldv_{k+1},\dots, \boldv_n)\text{,}\) using Theorem 1.18.8. Observe that \(\dim\NS T=\nullity T=k\) and \(\dim V=n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We claim that \(B''=(T(\boldv_{k+1}),T(\boldv_{k+2}),\dots, T(\boldv_{n}))\) is a basis of \(\im T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First we show that \(B''\) is linearly independent. Suppose \(a_kT(\boldv_k)+a_{k+1}T(\boldv_{k+1})+\cdotss +a_nT(\boldv_n)=\boldzero\text{.}\) Then the vector \(\boldv=a_k\boldv_k+a_{k+1}\boldv_{k+1}+\cdots +a_n\boldv_n\) satisfies \(T(\boldv)=\boldzero\) (using linearity of \(T\)), and hence \(\boldv\in \NS T\text{.}\) Then, using the fact that \(B'\) is a basis of \(\NS T\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} b_1\boldv_1+b_2\boldv_2+\cdots +\boldv_k=\boldv=a_k\boldv_k+a_{k+1}\boldv_{k+1}+\cdots +a_n\boldv_n, \end{equation*}
    and hence
    \begin{equation*} b_1\boldv_1+b_2\boldv_2+\cdots +\boldv_k-a_k\boldv_k-a_{k+1}\boldv_{k+1}-\cdots -a_n\boldv_n=\boldzero. \end{equation*}
    Since the set \(B\) is linearly independent, we conclude that \(b_i=a_j=0\) for all \(1\leq i\leq k\) and \(k+1\leq j\leq n\text{.}\) In particular, \(a_{k+1}=a_{k+2}=\cdots=a_n=0\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    First observe that since \(T(\boldv_i)\in \im T\) for all \(k+1\leq i\leq n\text{,}\) it follows from Theorem 1.15.9 that
    \begin{equation*} \Span (T(\boldv_i))_{i=k+1}^n=\Span B''\subseteq im T\text{.} \end{equation*}
    For the other direction, suppose \(\boldw\in \im T\text{.}\) Then there is a \(\boldv\in V\) such that \(\boldw=T(\boldv)\text{.}\) Since \(B\) is a basis of \(V\) we may write
    \begin{equation*} \boldv=a_1\boldv_1+a_2\boldv_2+\cdots a_k\boldv_k+a_{k+1}\boldv_{k+1}+\cdots +a_n\boldv_n\text{,} \end{equation*}
    in which case
    \begin{align*} \boldw=T(\boldv)\amp= T(a_1\boldv_1+a_2\boldv_2+\cdots a_k\boldv_k+a_{k+1}\boldv_{k+1}+\cdots +a_n\boldv_n)\\ \amp=a_1T(\boldv_1)+a_2T(\boldv_2)+\cdots a_kT(\boldv_k)+a_{k+1}T(\boldv_{k+1})+\cdots +a_nT(\boldv_n) \amp (T \text{ is linear })\\ \amp=\boldzero +a_kT(\boldv_k)+a_{k+1}T(\boldv_{k+1})+\cdots +a_nT(\boldv_n) \amp (\boldv_i\in\NS T \text{ for } 1\leq i\leq k) \text{.} \end{align*}
    This shows that \(\boldw=a_kT(\boldv_k)+a_{k+1}T(\boldv_{k+1})+\cdots +a_nT(\boldv_n)\in \Span B''\text{,}\) as desired.
    🔗
    Having shown \(B''\) is a basis for \(\im T\text{,}\) we conclude that \(\dim \im T=\val{B''}=n-(k+1)+1=n-k\text{,}\) and thus that
    \begin{align*} \dim V \amp=k+(n-k) \\ \amp=\dim\NS T+\dim \im T \\ \amp = \nullity T+\rank T\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The rank-nullity theorem relates the size (measured by dimension) of the two spaces \(\NS T\) and \(\im T\text{.}\) This can be useful in computations when one of these two spaces is easy to compute.

    Corollary 1.20.15 Injective, surjective, bijective.

    Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space, and let \(T\colon V\rightarrow W\) be a linear transformation.
    1. If \(\dim V> \dim W\text{,}\) then \(T\) is not injective.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(\dim V< \dim W\text{,}\) then \(T\) is not surjective.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Assume further that \(\dim V=\dim W< \infty\text{.}\) The following statements are equivalent.
      1. \(T\) is bijective.
        🔗
        🔗
      2. \(T\) is injective.
        🔗
        🔗
      3. \(T\) is surjective.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    1. First assume \(\dim V=n< \infty\text{.}\) We have
      \begin{align*} \dim \NS T \amp =\dim V-\dim\im T \amp (\text{rank-nullity})\\ \amp > \dim W-\dim \im T \amp (\dim V> \dim W)\\ \amp \geq 0 \amp (\dim \im T\leq \dim W)\text{.} \end{align*}
      It follows that \(\dim \NS T> 0\text{,}\) and hence that \(\NS T\ne \{\boldzero\}\text{,}\) and thus \(T\) is not injective.
      🔗
      Now assume that \(\dim V=\infty\) and \(\dim W=m< \infty\text{.}\) Pick an infinite linearly independent tuple \((v_i)_{i\in i}\) in \(V\text{,}\) and let \(B=(v_{i_1}, v_{i_2},\dots, v_{i_n})\) be any finite subtuple of length \(n> m\text{.}\) We know from a homework exercise that \(B\) is linearly independent. Thus \(V'=\Span B\) is an \(n\)-dimensional subspace of \(V\) and satisfies \(\dim V'> \dim W\text{.}\) Applying the previous argument to the restriction \(T\vert_{V'}\colon V'\rightarrow W\text{,}\) we conclude that \(\NS T\vert_{V'}\ne\{\boldzero\}\text{.}\) Since \(\NS T\vert_{V'}\subseteq \NS T\text{,}\) we conclude that \(\NS T\ne \{\boldzero\}\text{,}\) and thus \(T\) is not injective.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We assume \(\dim V=n< \dim W\text{.}\) Since
      \begin{equation*} \dim \im T=\dim V-\dim \NS T\leq \dim V< \dim W \end{equation*}
      we cannot have have \(\im T=\dim W\text{.}\) Thus \(T\) is not surjective.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. We assume \(\dim V=\dim W=n< \infty\text{.}\) We prove a cycle of implications (a)\(\implies\)(b)\(\implies\)(c)\(\implies\)(d).
      🔗
      We have (a) implies (b) by definition.
      🔗
      Assume \(T\) is injective. By Theorem 1.20.11, this implies \(\NS T=\{\boldzero\}\) and hence \(\dim \NS T=0\text{.}\) It follows that
      \begin{equation*} \dim \im T=\dim V-\dim \NS T=\dim V-0=\dim W\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Since \(\im W\subseteq W\) and \(\dim \im W=\dim W\text{,}\) we have \(\im T=W\) by Corollary 1.18.10. Thus \(T\) is surjective. This proves that (b) implies (c).
      🔗
      🔗
    4. If \(T\) is surjective, then \(\im T=W\) in which case \(\dim \im T=\dim W=n\text{.}\) It follows that
      \begin{equation*} \dim \NS T=\dim V-\dim \im T=n-n=0\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Hence \(\dim \NS T=0\text{,}\) or equivalently, \(\NS T=\{\boldzero\}\text{.}\) It follows that \(T\) is injective. Since \(T\) is injective and surjective, it is bijective. This proves that (c) implies (a).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
