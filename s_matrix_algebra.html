    Section 1.10 Matrix algebra

    The last section was devoted to what might be called the arithmetic of matrices. We learned the basic operations of adding, multiplying, scaling, and transposing matrices. In this section we tackle the algebra of matrices. We will investigate the properties enjoyed (and not enjoyed) by our matrix operations, and will show how to use these operations to solve matrix equations.
    🔗
    As you learn about matrix algebra, always keep in mind your old friend, real number algebra. For the most part these two algebraic systems closely resemble one another, as Theorem 1.10.1 below makes clear. However, there are two crucial points where they differ (see Theorem 1.10.10): two important properties of real number algebra that do not hold for matrices. The consequences of these two simple aberrations are far-reaching and imbue matrix algebra with a fascinating richness in comparison to real number algebra.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.1 Matrix multiplication properties

    We begin with a few results that establish a close connection between matrix multiplication and real number multiplication.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.10.1. Properties of matrix multiplication.

    In what follows \(m,n,r,s\) will denote positive integers.
    1. Associative law.
      \(A(BC)=(AB)C\) for all \(A\in \ML_{m,r}( F), B\in \ML_{r,s}( F), C\in \ML_{s,n}( F)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Left-distributive law.
      \(A(B+C)=AB+AC\) for all \(A\in \ML_{m,r}( F)\) and \(B,C\in \ML_{r,n}( F)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Right-distributive law.
      \((B+C)A=BA+CA\) for all \(B,C\in \ML_{m,r}( F)\) and \(A\in \ML_{r,n}( F)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Scaling commutative law.
      \(a(BC)=(aB)C=B(aC)\) for all \(a\in F\text{,}\) \(B\in \ML_{m,r}( F)\text{,}\) \(C\in \ML_{r,n}( F)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    How does one actually prove one of these properties? These are all matrix equalities of the form \(A=B\text{,}\) and according to Proposition 1.9.6, two matrices are equal if (a) they are of the same shape, and (b) their corresponding entries are equal. Let’s make an official procedure out of this, using matrix-entry notation.
    🔗

    Procedure 1.10.2. Proving matrix equalities.

    Let \(A\) be an \(m\times n\) matrix, and let \(B\) be an \(m'\times n'\) matrix. To show that \(A=B\) proceed as follows.
    1. Same dimensions.
      Show that \(m=m'\) and \(n=n'\text{:}\) i.e., show that \(A\) and \(B\) have the same shape.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Same entries.
      Having shown that both matrices are of dimension \(m\times n\text{,}\) show that
      \begin{align} [A]_{i,j} \amp =[B]_{i,j}\tag{1.10.1} \end{align}
      for all \(1\leq i\leq m, 1\leq j\leq n\text{.}\) Equivalently, if \(A=[a_{i,j}]_{m\times n}\) and \(B=[b_{i,j}]_{m\times n}\text{,}\) show that \(a_{i,j}=b_{i,j}\) for all \(1\leq i\leq m, 1\leq j\leq n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s illustrate Procedure 1.10.2 by proving the multiplication associative law of Theorem 1.10.1.
    🔗

    Proof of Theorem 1.10.1: associative law.

    We prove only the associative law. Let \(A=[a_{ij}]_{m\times r}\text{,}\) \(B=[b_{ij}]_{r\times s}\text{,}\) \(C=[c_{ij}]_{s\times n}\text{.}\) To show
    \begin{equation*} A(BC)=(AB)C\text{,} \end{equation*}
    we must show (1) that \(A(BC)\) and \((AB)C\) have the same dimension, and (2) that
    \begin{equation*} [A(BC)]_{ij}=[(AB)C]_{ij} \end{equation*}
    for all possible \((i,j)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Addressing the dimensions of the left and right sides, note that since \(A\in \ML_{m,r}( F), B\in \ML_{r,s}, C\in \ML_{s,n}( F)\text{,}\) we have (using Definition 1.9.8)
    \begin{align*} AB \amp \in \ML_{m,s}( F) \amp BC\amp \in \ML_{s,n}( F)\\ A(BC) \amp \in \ML_{m,n}( F) \amp (AB)C\amp \in \ML_{m,n}( F)\text{.} \end{align*}
    Thus \(A(BC)\) and \((AB)C\) are both \(m\times n\) matrices.
    🔗
    Next, given any \((i,j)\) with \(1\leq i\leq m\) and \(1\leq j\leq n\text{,}\) we have:
    \begin{align*} [A(BC)]_{ij}\amp=\sum_{\ell=1}^ra_{i\ell}[BC]_{\ell j} \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/d_matrix_mult.html}{\text{Definition 1.9.8}})\\ \amp=\sum_{\ell=1}^ra_{i\ell}\left(\sum_{k=1}^sb_{\ell k}c_{kj}\right) \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/d_matrix_mult.html}{\text{Definition 1.9.8}})\\ =\amp \sum_{\ell=1}^r\sum_{k=1}^sa_{i\ell}(b_{\ell k}c_{kj}) \amp \text{ (real dist.) }\\ =\amp \sum_{k=1}^s\sum_{\ell=1}^r(a_{i\ell}b_{\ell k})c_{kj} \amp \text{ (real comm., real assoc.) }\\ =\amp \sum_{k=1}^s\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^ra_{i\ell}b_{\ell k}\right)c_{kj} \amp \text{ (real dist.) }\\ =\amp \sum_{k=1}^s[AB]_{ik}c_{kj} =\Bigl[(AB)C]_{ij} \end{align*}
    🔗
    This proves that all entries of the two matrices are equal, and hence \(A(BC)=(AB)C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.10.3. Video example: proving matrix equalities.

    🔗
    Like real number algebra, there are certain matrices that satisfy special properties with regard to the operations of addition and multiplication. We have seen examples of this already thanks to the natural vector space structure of \(\ML_{m,n}(F)\text{.}\) For example, the \(m\times n\) zero matrix \(\boldzero_{m\times n}\) acts as an additive indentity (i.e., \(\boldzero_{m\times n}+A=A\) for all \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\)) and vector inverses \(-A\) act as additive inverses (i.e., \(-A+A=\boldzero_{m\times n}\) for all \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\)). We now introduce the identity matrices \(I_n\text{,}\) which satisfy a multiplicative identity property with respect to matrix multiplication, and thus play a role analogous to the number \(1\) among real numbers.
    🔗

    Definition 1.10.5. Identity matrix.

    The \(n\times n\) identity matrix, denoted \(I_n\text{,}\) is the square \(n\times n\) matrix
    \begin{equation*} I_n=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\amp 0 \amp \dots\amp 0\\ 0\amp 1 \amp 0 \amp \dots \amp 0\\ 0\amp 0\amp 1 \amp \dots \amp 0\\ \vdots \amp \vdots \amp \amp \ddots \amp \vdots\\ 0\amp 0\amp \dots \amp 0\amp 1\end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    with ones along the diagonal and zeros everywhere else. In other words, for all \(1\leq i\leq n\) and \(1\leq j\leq n\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} [I_n]_{i,j}=\begin{cases} 1 \amp \text{if } i=j \\ 0\amp \text{if } i\ne j \text{.}\end{cases} \end{equation*}
    When there is no confusion about which dimension identity matrix is intended, we will often write simply \(I\) instead of \(I_n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 1.10.6. Multiplicative identities.

    Let \(n\) be a positive integer. The \(n\times n\) identity matrix acts as a multiplicative identity among \(n\times n\) matrices in the following sense: for all \(A\in \ML_{n,n}(F)\) we have
    \begin{equation*} I_n\, A=A\, I_n=A\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Left as an exercise.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.10.2 Cancellation laws fulfilled and violated

    When performing real number algebra, we frequently make use of cancellation laws, and these laws come in a variety of flavors. For example, the additive cancellation law asserts that
    \begin{equation*} x+y=x+z \iff y=z \end{equation*}
    for all \(x,y,z\in \R\text{.}\) Similarly, the multiplicative cancellation law asserts that for any nonzero \(x\in \R\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} xy=xz \iff y=z \end{equation*}
    for all \(y,z\in \R\text{.}\) We now investigate the extent to which these laws apply in matrix algebra. The results, as you will see, are mixed!
    🔗

    Theorem 1.10.7. Additive cancellation of matrices.

    Given \(m\times n\) matrices \(A, B\text{,}\) and \(C\text{,}\) we have \(A+B=A+C\) if and only if \(B=C\text{.}\) Using logical notation:
    \begin{equation*} A+B=A+C \iff B=C. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    As simple as this claim might seem, remember that we are dealing with a completely new algebraic system here. We will prove both implications of “if and only if” statement separately.
    🔗
    Proof: \(A+B=A+C\implies B=C\).
    We prove this via a chain of implications:
    \begin{align*} A+B=A+C\amp \implies -A+(A+B)=-A+(A+C) \\ \amp\implies (-A+A)+B=(-A+A)+C \\ \amp\implies \boldzero_{m\times n}+B=\boldzero_{m\times n}+C \\ \amp \implies B=C \text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Proof: \(B=C\implies A+B=A+C\).
    This direction is obvious: if \(B\) and \(C\) are equal matrices, then they remain equal when we add \(A\) to each of them.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.10.8.

    The algebraic importance of Theorem 1.10.7 is that we can perform additive cancellation in matrix equations just as we do in real number algebra. For example, we can solve the matrix equation \(A+B=3A\) for \(B\) as follows:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} A+B\amp = 3A\\ A+B\amp = (1+2)A\\ A+B\amp = A+2A\\ B\amp = 2A \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/c_matrix_additive_canc.html}{\text{Theorem 1.10.7}})\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Warning 1.10.9.

    Though we can perform additive cancellation in matrix algebra, we can not always perform multiplicative cancellation. For example, consider the matrices
    \begin{equation*} A=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\\ 1\amp 1\end{bmatrix}, B=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, C=\begin{bmatrix}0\amp 0\\ 1\amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Check for yourself that \(AB=AC\text{,}\) and yet \(B\ne C\text{.}\) In other words, we cannot always “cancel” \(A\) from the matrix equation \(AB=AC\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The example in our warning above is but one instance of the general failure of the principle of multiplicative cancellation in matrix algebra. This in turn is a consequence of the following theorem, which identifies the two crucial places where matrix algebra differs significantly from real number algebra.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.10.10. Matrix algebra abnormalities.

    1. Matrix multiplication is not commutative.
      Given \(n\times n\) matrices \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) we do not necessarily have \(AB=BA\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Products of nonzero matrices may be equal to zero.
      If the product of two matrices is the zero matrix, we cannot conclude that one of matrices is the zero matrix. In logical notation:
      \begin{equation} \underset{m\times n}{A}\, \underset{n\times r}{B}=\boldzero_{m\times r}\;\not\!\!\!\!\implies A=\boldzero_{m\times n} \text{ or } B=\boldzero_{n\times r}\text{.}\tag{1.10.2} \end{equation}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    This is a good place to point out that to prove an identity does not hold, it suffices to provide a single counterexample to that effect. We do so for each failed identity of Theorem 1.10.10 in turn. There is no significance to the particular counterexamples chosen here, and indeed there are infinitely many counterexamples to choose from in both cases.
    1. We have
      \begin{align*} \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}0\amp 1\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\amp = \begin{bmatrix} 0\amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 0\end{bmatrix}\\ \begin{bmatrix}0\amp 1\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \amp = \begin{bmatrix} 0\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{align*}
      and thus
      \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}0\amp 1\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \ne \begin{bmatrix}0\amp 1\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Observe that
      \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix}0\amp 1\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}0\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \text{.} \end{equation*}
      This is an example of two nonzero matrices whose product is the zero matrix.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    An important consequence of the abnormality (1.10.2) is that matrix algebra does not enjoy the property of multiplicative cancellation.
    🔗

    Corollary 1.10.11. Failure of multiplicative cancellation.

    1. Suppose matrices \(A, B, C\) satisfy \(AB=AC\) and \(A\ne \boldzero\text{.}\) We cannot conclude that \(B=C\text{.}\) In logical notation:
      \begin{equation} AB=AC \;\not\!\!\!\!\implies B=C\tag{1.10.3} \end{equation}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose matrices \(B, C, D\) satisfy \(BD=CD\) and \(D\ne \boldzero\text{.}\) We cannot conclude that \(B=C\text{.}\) In logical notation:
      \begin{equation} BD=CD \;\not\!\!\!\!\implies B=C\tag{1.10.4} \end{equation}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Again, we need only provide explicit counterexamples for each statement.
    🔗
    1. Let \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(B=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(C=\begin{bmatrix}0\amp 0\\ 1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Verify for yourself that
      \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}0\amp 0\\ 1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix} 1\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Thus \(AB=AC\text{,}\) but clearly \(B\ne C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Let \(B=\begin{bmatrix}2\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(C=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(D=\begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\amp 1\\ 1\amp 1\amp 1 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) We have
      \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix}2\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\amp 1\\ 1\amp 1\amp 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\\ 0\amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1\amp 1\amp 1\\ 1\amp 1\amp 1 \end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix} 2\amp 2\amp 2\\ 2\amp 2\amp 2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Thus \(BD=CD\text{,}\) but \(B\ne C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.10.12.

    Mark well this important abnormality of matrix algebra. Confronted with a real number equation of the form \(ab=ac\text{,}\) we have a deeply ingrained impulse to declare that either \(a=0\) or \(b=c\text{.}\) (If we’re sloppy we may forget about that first possibility.) The corresponding maneuver for the matrix equation \(AB=AC\) is simply not available to us, unless we know something more about \(A\text{.}\)

    Subsection 1.10.3 Transpostition properties

    We end our foray into matrix algebra with some properties articulating how matrix transposition interacts with matrix addition, multiplication and scalar multiplication.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.10.13. Properties of matrix transposition.

    In what follows \(m,n,r\) will denote positive integers.
    1. \((A+B)^T=A^T+B^T\) for all \(A,B\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \((cA)^T=cA^T\) for all \(c\in F\) and \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \((AB)^T=B^TA^T\) for all \(A\in \ML_{m,r}(F), B\in \ML_{r,n}(F)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\left(A^T\right)^T=A\) for all \(A\in \ML_{m,n}(F)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We prove only the first statement. First observe that if \(A\) is \(m\times n\text{,}\) then so is \(B\) and \(A+B\text{.}\) Then \((A+B)^T\) is \(n\times m\) by Definition 1.9.18. Similarly, we see that \(A^T+B^T\) is \(n\times m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Next, given any \((i,j)\) with \(1\leq i\leq n\text{,}\) \(1\leq j\leq m\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{align*} \left((A+B)^T\right)_{ij}\amp =(A+B)_{ji} \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/d_transpose.html}{\text{Definition 1.9.18}})\\ \amp = A_{ji}+B_{ji} \amp \\ \amp =(A^T)_{ij}+(B^T)_{ij} \amp (\knowl{./knowl/xref/d_transpose.html}{\text{Definition 1.9.18}} \\ \amp =(A^T+B^T)_{ij} \amp \text{.} \end{align*}
    Since the \(ij\)-entries of both matrices are equal for each \((i,j)\text{,}\) it follows that \((A+B)^T=A^T+B^T\text{.}\)
